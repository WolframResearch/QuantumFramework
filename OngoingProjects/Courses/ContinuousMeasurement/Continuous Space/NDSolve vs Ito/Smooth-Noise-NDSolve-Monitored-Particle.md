# Handing NDSolve the smooth-noise measurement equation: a monitored particle on a grid

**A quantum particle whose position is continuously monitored obeys a stochastic Schrödinger equation, and the companion essay in this folder showed how to hand such an equation to `NDSolve` for a qubit: write it in Stratonovich form and replace the white noise by a smooth interpolated Wiener path. This document carries the same trick into continuous space, where the state is a wavefunction on a grid and the equation is a stochastic partial differential equation. The route has one genuinely new obstacle: the physical equation's coefficients contain $\langle\hat{x}\rangle$, an integral of the unknown, and `NDSolve` cannot discretize an integral of the unknown. The fix is to integrate the linear, unnormalized form of the equation, whose coefficients are the bare operators $\hat{x}$ and $\hat{x}^2$, and to read the physics through the decaying norm, which is not a defect but the likelihood of the record. Every step is certified in place: the Stratonovich damping is verified symbolically, the noise-off solver is held to the exact free and coherent evolutions, the conditional covariance flow is solved in closed form and one noisy trajectory is held to it pathwise, its two driven means matched against the same noise path through exact gains, the deliberately wrong Itô damping visibly misses, the ensemble rebuilds the Lindblad master equation and the law of total variance, one record generated by the split-step engine of the parent essay is filtered through `NDSolve` to the same conditional state, and the route then leaves the quadratic comfort zone entirely, collapsing a double-well cat with only the model-free guards on duty.**

## Part I: The Measurement Equation, and the Coefficient NDSolve Cannot Hold

Recall the equation of motion from the parent essay on measurement in a potential. A particle with Hamiltonian $\hat H=\hat p^2/2m+V(\hat x)$ whose position is monitored continuously and weakly obeys the stochastic Schrödinger equation

$$
d|\psi\rangle = \left[-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\hat H\,dt \;-\; \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\bigl(\hat x - \langle\hat x\rangle\bigr)^2 dt \;+\; \sqrt{\lambda}\,\bigl(\hat x - \langle\hat x\rangle\bigr)\,dW\right]|\psi\rangle ,
$$

where $\lambda$ sets how fast position information arrives and $dW$ is a Wiener increment. The detector's output over the same increment is the measurement record $dY_t = 2\sqrt{\lambda}\,\langle\hat x\rangle_t\,dt + dW_t$, the conditional mean position buried in shot noise. The parent essay integrates this equation with a hand-built engine: a Bayesian measurement update alternating with a split-step Fourier drift. Here we ask a different question: can `NDSolve` swallow the equation whole, discretizing space itself, the way it does for an ordinary partial differential equation?

Not as written, and the obstacle is worth seeing precisely. `NDSolve`'s method of lines turns a partial differential equation into coupled ordinary differential equations by sampling every coefficient at grid points. The coefficient $\langle\hat x\rangle=\int x\,|\psi(x,t)|^2\,dx$ is not a function of the point $x$: it is an integral of the unknown over the whole line, a single number that depends on the entire current solution. In other words, the equation is nonlocal in exactly the place a spatial discretizer needs locality. Confirm that `NDSolve` refuses the nonlocal equation, returning unevaluated while `NIntegrate` reports that it cannot sample the unknown (the two messages below are the refusal, not an accident of ours):

```wl
Head[NDSolveValue[{D[u[x, t], t] == (I/2) D[u[x, t], {x, 2}] -
      NIntegrate[y Abs[u[y, t]]^2, {y, -5, 5}] u[x, t],
    u[x, 0] == E^(-x^2)}, u, {t, 0, 0.1}, {x, -5, 5}]] === NDSolveValue
```

As one can see, the call comes back untouched, with one complaint from each layer: the integral cannot sample the still-symbolic unknown, and `NDSolve` classifies what remains as a kind of equation it does not support. The gate is real, and no method option opens it. So if `NDSolve` is to integrate this physics, the equation itself must change.

The change is standard in quantum filtering, and it is a simplification, not an approximation. Drop the two $\langle\hat x\rangle$ subtractions and carry an unnormalized state $|\tilde\psi\rangle$:

$$
d|\tilde\psi\rangle = \left[-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\hat H \;-\; \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\,\hat x^2\right]|\tilde\psi\rangle\,dt \;+\; \sqrt{\lambda}\,\hat x\,|\tilde\psi\rangle\,dY_t .
$$

Every coefficient is now a pointwise function of $x$, exactly what a spatial discretizer wants, and the equation is linear in $|\tilde\psi\rangle$. Two things happened in exchange, and they are the two facts the rest of this document leans on. First, the equation is driven by the record $Y$ itself, not by the noise: the linear equation is a filter, a machine that eats a record and produces the state consistent with it. We will feed it records from two different sources, a plain Wiener path in Parts III through V and a record generated by an entirely different integrator in Part VI. Second, the state no longer stays normalized, and the norm is not garbage: $\||\tilde\psi\rangle\|^2$ is the likelihood of the record that drove it, the probability weight that turns averages over plain Wiener paths into averages over physical records. In other words, normalization was carrying the statistics all along, and the linear form simply makes it visible. The normalized state is recovered at any moment as $|\psi\rangle=|\tilde\psi\rangle/\||\tilde\psi\rangle\|$.

Both facts are one algebraic identity away, so let us certify them before trusting them. Averaging the outer product $\tilde\rho=\mathbb{E}[|\tilde\psi\rangle\langle\tilde\psi|]$ over records that are plain Wiener paths, the Itô rule gives $d\tilde\rho/dt = A\tilde\rho+\tilde\rho A^\dagger + B\tilde\rho B^\dagger$ with $A=-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\hat H-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\hat x^2$ and $B=\sqrt{\lambda}\,\hat x$, and that combination should be the Lindblad master equation of unmonitored position measurement, $-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}[\hat H,\tilde\rho]+\lambda\left(\hat x\tilde\rho\hat x-\tfrac12\{\hat x^2,\tilde\rho\}\right)$. Verify the identity on a three-point grid with every entry symbolic, the Hamiltonian an arbitrary Hermitian matrix and the position operator an arbitrary real diagonal:

```wl
ClearAll[lam, hb, h11, h22, h33, h12, h13, h23, x1, x2, x3, r];
With[{ham = {{h11, h12, h13}, {Conjugate[h12], h22, h23},
    {Conjugate[h13], Conjugate[h23], h33}},
  xop = DiagonalMatrix[{x1, x2, x3}], rho = Array[r, {3, 3}]},
 With[{a = -I ham/hb - (lam/2) xop . xop, b = Sqrt[lam] xop},
  Simplify[(a . rho + rho . ConjugateTranspose[a] + b . rho . ConjugateTranspose[b]) ==
    (-I (ham . rho - rho . ham)/hb +
      lam (xop . rho . xop - (xop . xop . rho + rho . xop . xop)/2)),
   Element[{x1, x2, x3, h11, h22, h33}, Reals] && lam > 0 && hb > 0]]]
```

So the unweighted average of the outer products obeys the Lindblad equation, with no likelihood weight in sight: any observable linear in the density matrix can be averaged directly from the raw, unnormalized states. Taking the trace of the same identity kills the commutator and the dissipator termwise, so $\mathbb{E}\,\||\tilde\psi\rangle\|^2$ is conserved: the likelihood weight averages to one for all time, a property we will use in Part V as the loudest possible alarm bell, since a wrong drift sends this average off exponentially. Where the weight genuinely matters is for quantities nonlinear in the conditional state, the spread of the conditional mean across trajectories being the example Part V works out.

## Part II: The Linear Equation in Stratonovich Form: the Damping Doubles

`NDSolve` integrates ordinary calculus, so the white noise must become a function. The companion qubit essay establishes the recipe: draw a Wiener path on a grid, interpolate it into a smooth curve $W(t)$, and hand the solver $W'(t)$ as an ordinary forcing. The price is a change of stochastic calculus. A smooth curve of vanishing correlation time integrates to the Stratonovich reading of the equation, not the Itô one, so the drift we hand `NDSolve` must be the Stratonovich drift, with the conversion term already inside.

For this equation the conversion is one line of algebra, because the diffusion is linear in the state: $b(|\tilde\psi\rangle)=B|\tilde\psi\rangle$ with $B=\sqrt{\lambda}\,\hat x$. The Itô-to-Stratonovich correction subtracts half the derivative of the diffusion along itself, and for a linear diffusion that derivative is $B$ again, so the correction is exactly $\tfrac12 B^2=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\hat x^2$ at every grid size, with nothing to expand and nothing to truncate. Subtracting it from the Itô drift doubles the damping:

$$
d|\tilde\psi\rangle = \left[-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\hat H \;-\; \lambda\,\hat x^2\right]|\tilde\psi\rangle\,dt \;+\; \sqrt{\lambda}\,\hat x\,|\tilde\psi\rangle\circ dY_t .
$$

In other words, the entire difference between the equation `NDSolve` sees and the Itô equation of Part I is the coefficient of $\hat x^2$: $\lambda$ in place of $\lambda/2$. That factor of two is the whole design, so it must not rest on our algebra alone. Verify it with the built-in conversion, on a three-point grid with the strength and the grid points symbolic: hand `StratonovichProcess` the doubled damping and the linear diffusion, convert to the Itô form, and compare against the Itô drift of Part I:

```wl
ClearAll[p1, p2, p3, a1, a2, a3, t];
FullSimplify[ItoProcess[StratonovichProcess[
     {{-lam x1^2 p1[t], -lam x2^2 p2[t], -lam x3^2 p3[t]},
      List /@ {Sqrt[lam] x1 p1[t], Sqrt[lam] x2 p2[t], Sqrt[lam] x3 p3[t]},
      {p1[t], p2[t], p3[t]}},
     {{p1, p2, p3}, {a1, a2, a3}}, t]]["Drift"] ==
  {-(lam/2) x1^2 p1[t], -(lam/2) x2^2 p2[t], -(lam/2) x3^2 p3[t]}]
```

The measurement terms are diagonal, so each grid component carries the conversion independently and three symbolic points certify the pattern for any number of them; the Hamiltonian, being noise-free, passes through the conversion untouched. Note what did not need converting: the record itself. The relation $dY=2\sqrt{\lambda}\langle\hat x\rangle dt+dW$ has additive noise, so $Y$ means the same thing in both calculi, exactly as the qubit essay found for its readout row.

## Part III: Encoding: the Grid, the Smooth Noise, and One NDSolve Call

We now build the machinery, reusing the parent essay's conventions so the two documents remain one toolbox. Fix units so that $\hbar=m=1$:

```wl
hbar = 1; mass = 1;
```

The grid is the parent essay's: $n$ points across a box of length $l$ glued into a ring, with the quantized momenta stored beside the positions in the order the discrete Fourier transform uses them:

```wl
ClearAll[grid];
grid[n_, l_] := With[{dx = l/n},
   <|"n" -> n, "dx" -> dx, "x" -> dx Range[-n/2, n/2 - 1],
     "p" -> (2 Pi hbar/l) RotateLeft[Range[-n/2, n/2 - 1], n/2]|>];
```

Carry over the transform pair and the two leakage guards. The box is a ring, so probability that reaches the wall wraps to the far side, and the momentum ladder has a top rung, so probability that reaches the fastest momenta aliases into slow motion; each guard measures how much probability sits in the outer tenth of its axis:

```wl
ClearAll[ft, ift, outerTenth, edgeMass, tailMass];
ft[psi_] := Fourier[psi, FourierParameters -> {0, -1}];
ift[c_] := InverseFourier[c, FourierParameters -> {0, -1}];
outerTenth[coord_, probs_] := Total@Pick[probs, UnitStep[Abs[coord] - 0.9 Max@Abs[coord]], 1];
edgeMass[g_, psi_] := outerTenth[g["x"], Abs[psi]^2];
tailMass[g_, psi_] := outerTenth[g["p"], Abs[ft[psi]]^2];
```

The initial state is the parent's Gaussian packet, in two forms for the two engines it will feed: the grid vector, normalized so its absolute squares sum to one (each entry carries a factor $\sqrt{dx}$, making expectations plain dot products), and the same packet as a continuum function of $x$ with the integral normalization $(2\pi s^2)^{-1/4}e^{-(x-x_0)^2/4s^2+ip_0x/\hbar}$, which is the form `NDSolve` takes as an initial condition. Writing that continuum normalization once, as a helper, means no hand-typed prefactor appears again below:

```wl
ClearAll[gaussian, packet, xMean, xVar];
gaussian[g_, x0_, p0_, s_] := Normalize[Exp[-(g["x"] - x0)^2/(4 s^2) + I p0 g["x"]/hbar]];
packet[x0_, p0_, s_] := Function[x, E^(-(x - x0)^2/(4 s^2) + I p0 x/hbar)/(2 Pi s^2)^(1/4)];
xMean[g_, psi_] := g["x"] . Abs[psi]^2;
xVar[g_, psi_] := g["x"]^2 . Abs[psi]^2 - xMean[g, psi]^2;
```

Now the noise. Draw independent Gaussian increments with variance the grid spacing on a uniform time grid, accumulate them into a Wiener path, and interpolate with a cubic, so that $W(t)$ is a continuous curve and $W'(t)$ an ordinary piecewise-smooth function; this is the companion essay's recipe verbatim, and the seed makes every path reproducible:

```wl
ClearAll[wienerPath];
wienerPath[tf_, dtn_, seed_] := BlockRandom[SeedRandom[seed];
   With[{knots = Subdivide[0., tf, Max[3, Ceiling[tf/dtn]]]},
    Interpolation[Transpose[{knots,
       Prepend[Accumulate[RandomVariate[
          NormalDistribution[0, Sqrt[knots[[2]] - knots[[1]]]], Length[knots] - 1]], 0.]}],
     InterpolationOrder -> 3]]];
```

Visualize one realization of the path and the ordinary function that stands in for white noise:

```wl
With[{wf = wienerPath[2., 0.005, 7]},
 GraphicsRow[{
   Plot[wf[t], {t, 0, 2}, Frame -> True, GridLines -> Automatic,
    PlotLabel -> "smooth Wiener path W(t)", FrameLabel -> {"t", "W"}],
   Plot[wf'[t], {t, 0, 2}, Frame -> True, GridLines -> Automatic,
    PlotLabel -> "its derivative W'(t): the smooth noise", FrameLabel -> {"t", "W'"}]},
  ImageSize -> 580]]
```

As one can see, the path is a continuous random walk and its derivative is jagged but genuine, varying on the scale of the drawing grid. That drawing step is the physics dial of the whole construction: it fixes the correlation time of the smoothed noise, hence which stochastic equation we are actually solving, converging to the ideal white-noise equation only as it shrinks; we return to it with a sweep in Part V.

One decision remains: how `NDSolve` should discretize space. Three findings, each established by running the alternative rather than by trusting a manual, pin the choice. First, the built-in `PeriodicBoundaryCondition` always routes the problem to the finite element discretizer, whatever method options accompany it; the finite element route does integrate this equation, but two orders of magnitude slower and less accurately than what follows, so we reject it on cost. Second, on a periodic problem the classic idiom is to state periodicity as an equation, $u(-l/2,t)=u(l/2,t)$, which keeps the fast tensor-product grid available. Third, on this stochastic forcing the tensor grid needs its coordinates pinned explicitly (left to choose its own grid, the solver stalls), and pinning them to our own grid plus the duplicated endpoint has a bonus: the solver's samples land exactly on our grid points, so reading the state back involves no interpolation at all. Assemble the one call, taking the noise as any interpolated path; the potential enters as one pointwise factor, so any $V(x)$ rides along. Two named options cover the two knobs later Parts will turn: the solver's step cap, by default a quarter of the path's knot spacing so no step straddles a knot, and a damping factor held at one, which exists so that the deliberately wrong Itô coefficient of Part IV will be literally one number in one call, not a retyped solver whose transcription would itself need auditing:

```wl
ClearAll[smoothNoiseTrajectory];
Options[smoothNoiseTrajectory] = {"StepCap" -> Automatic, "DampingFactor" -> 1};
smoothNoiseTrajectory[g_, vfun_, lam_, psi0_, tf_, wf_, OptionsPattern[]] :=
  Module[{u, x, t},
   With[{a = g["n"] g["dx"]/2., knot = wf["Coordinates"][[1, 2]] - wf["Coordinates"][[1, 1]],
     damp = OptionValue["DampingFactor"]},
    NDSolveValue[{D[u[x, t], t] == (I hbar/(2 mass)) D[u[x, t], {x, 2}] -
        (I/hbar) vfun[x] u[x, t] - damp lam x^2 u[x, t] + Sqrt[lam] x u[x, t] wf'[t],
      u[x, 0] == psi0[x], u[-a, t] == u[a, t]},
     u, {t, 0, tf}, {x, -a, a},
     MaxStepSize -> If[OptionValue["StepCap"] === Automatic, knot/4, OptionValue["StepCap"]],
     Method -> {"MethodOfLines", "SpatialDiscretization" ->
        {"TensorProductGrid", "Coordinates" -> {Append[g["x"], a]},
         "DifferenceOrder" -> 4}}]]];
```

The solution comes back as a function of $x$ and $t$, and we read it on our own grid: sampling at the grid points and multiplying by $\sqrt{dx}$ lands the state in the parent essay's vector convention, except that its total weight now carries the likelihood instead of being pinned at one. Define the sampler and the weight:

```wl
ClearAll[sampleState, norm2, conditional];
sampleState[g_, sol_, t_] := Sqrt[g["dx"]] sol[g["x"], t];
norm2[v_] := Total[Abs[v]^2];
conditional[g_, sol_, t_] := Normalize[sampleState[g, sol, t]];
```

Every conditional quantity is then the parent's accessor applied to the normalized sample. Before any noise, hold the solver to the two evolutions we know exactly. With the measurement off, a free Gaussian of width $s_0$ spreads so its position variance grows as $s_0^2+(\hbar t/2ms_0)^2$; run it and compare the endpoint against that closed form, and the weight against one:

```wl
gF = grid[128, 20.]; ClearAll[flat]; flat[x_] := 0 x; s0F = 2.; tfF = 2.;
solFree = smoothNoiseTrajectory[gF, flat, 0., packet[0., 0., s0F],
   tfF, wienerPath[tfF, 0.005, 7]];
{Abs[xVar[gF, conditional[gF, solFree, tfF]] - (s0F^2 + (hbar tfF/(2 mass s0F))^2)],
 Abs[norm2[sampleState[gF, solFree, tfF]] - 1]}
```

Both residuals are far below every effect we care about, and the first deserves to be split, because its two parts have different owners. The exact evolution on the ring costs one diagonal multiply in the momentum basis, with no solver at all; measure how far the solver's final variance sits from that exact ring propagation:

```wl
Abs[xVar[gF, conditional[gF, solFree, tfF]] -
  xVar[gF, Normalize[ift[Exp[-I gF["p"]^2 tfF/(2 mass hbar)] ft[sampleState[gF, solFree, 0.]]]]]]
```

The solver's own share is well over an order below the previous cell's residual: most of that residual belongs not to `NDSolve` but to the ring itself, the closed form describing an infinite line while the box folds the packet's spreading tails through the seam, exactly the effect `edgeMass` is built to meter. So the floor under everything downstream has two layers, a solver layer far down and a topology layer the guards watch, and nothing later will need to resolve past either. (With the strength at zero the noise term is multiplied away, so the same call exercises the whole machinery.) Now the trap. A coherent state, the ground-state Gaussian displaced sideways, must swing rigidly, its center tracing $x_0\cos\omega t$ with its width frozen at the ground-state value $\sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}$; run one and read the worst deviation of both facts over the window:

```wl
w0 = 0.8; x0C = 1.; ClearAll[vHarm]; vHarm[x_] := 0.5 mass w0^2 x^2;
solCoh = smoothNoiseTrajectory[gF, vHarm, 0.,
   packet[x0C, 0., Sqrt[hbar/(2 mass w0)]], 2 Pi/w0,
   wienerPath[2 Pi/w0, 0.005, 7]];
Max[Table[With[{v = conditional[gF, solCoh, tt]},
   Max[Abs[xMean[gF, v] - x0C Cos[w0 tt]], Abs[xVar[gF, v] - hbar/(2 mass w0)]]],
  {tt, Subdivide[0., 2 Pi/w0, 40]}]]
```

The center and the width both track the exact motion through a full period at the floor just priced, so the potential is wired correctly and the kinetic phases are resolved. This noise-off residual also identifies its owner. Fourth-order differences promise a specific reward for refining space, a sixteenfold drop per doubling of the grid; sweep the resolution on this same fixture and read the worst deviation at each. One adjustment as we do: the quarter-knot step cap exists to resolve the noise, and there is no noise here, so release it and let the adaptive error control own the step, which it already proved able to do on this fixture:

```wl
TableForm[Table[With[{gN = grid[n, 20.]},
   {n, Max[Table[With[{v = conditional[gN, smoothNoiseTrajectory[gN, vHarm, 0.,
          packet[x0C, 0., Sqrt[hbar/(2 mass w0)]], 2 Pi/w0,
          wienerPath[2 Pi/w0, 0.005, 7], "StepCap" -> 0.1], tt]},
       Max[Abs[xMean[gN, v] - x0C Cos[w0 tt]], Abs[xVar[gN, v] - hbar/(2 mass w0)]]],
      {tt, Subdivide[0., 2 Pi/w0, 40]}]]}], {n, {128, 256, 512}}],
 TableHeadings -> {None, {"n", "worst deviation"}}]
```

The first doubling collects nearly the full fourth-order reward; the second collects far less, the time integrator emerging as the new floor beneath the spatial one. So space owns this noise-off residual, and Part IV will show it owns almost none of the noisy one: three dials in this construction, each with its own jurisdiction. The machinery is certified quiet; time to switch the measurement on.

## Part IV: One Trajectory: Held to Exact References

What makes the monitored particle such a sharp test bed is a classical fact about Gaussian states under quadratic Hamiltonians: the conditional state stays Gaussian, and its five moments close among themselves. Better still, the three covariances decouple from the noise entirely. Writing $\Sigma_x$, $C$, $\Sigma_p$ for the position variance, the symmetrized covariance, and the momentum variance, they obey the deterministic flow

$$
\dot\Sigma_x=\frac{2C}{m}-4\lambda\,\Sigma_x^{2},\qquad
\dot C=\frac{\Sigma_p}{m}-m\omega^2\Sigma_x-4\lambda\,\Sigma_x C,\qquad
\dot\Sigma_p=-2m\omega^2C+\lambda\hbar^2-4\lambda\,C^{2},
$$

the same flow the parent essay solved for its steady state. Deterministic means pathwise: every single trajectory's width must ride this curve, whatever its noise does, which turns one run into an exact test with no ensemble in sight.

Two exact facts about this flow will serve as its credentials, so derive both before any integration. First the destination. At the fixed point all three rates vanish, three polynomial equations in three unknowns; solve them with every constant symbolic and keep the one branch a variance is allowed to occupy, the one that stays positive:

```wl
ClearAll[sxS, cS, spS, lamS, mS, hbS];
steadyF = FullSimplify[SelectFirst[
    SolveValues[{2 cS/mS - 4 lamS sxS^2 == 0, spS/mS - 4 lamS sxS cS == 0,
      lamS hbS^2 - 4 lamS cS^2 == 0}, {sxS, cS, spS}],
    TrueQ[Simplify[#[[1]] > 0 && #[[2]] > 0, {lamS > 0, mS > 0, hbS > 0}]] &],
   {lamS > 0, mS > 0, hbS > 0}]
```

As one can see, the correlation locks to $\hbar/2$ regardless of the measurement strength, while the position variance narrows with the strength. Make that scaling a computation rather than a remark: rescale the strength by an arbitrary factor and read what happens to the steady width:

```wl
Simplify[(Sqrt[steadyF[[1]]] /. lamS -> a lamS)/Sqrt[steadyF[[1]]],
 {lamS > 0, mS > 0, hbS > 0, a > 0}]
```

Watching harder narrows the steady width only as the fourth root of the strength: information about position is expensive at the bottom. Two more facts should follow at once, the width formula the parent essay states, and a sharper one, that the steady triple saturates the Robertson-Schrödinger floor $\Sigma_x\Sigma_p-C^2=\hbar^2/4$, because efficient monitoring keeps the state pure and a pure Gaussian sits exactly on that floor. Confirm both in one breath:

```wl
FullSimplify[{Sqrt[steadyF[[1]]] == (hbS/(4 lamS mS))^(1/4),
  steadyF[[1]] steadyF[[3]] - steadyF[[2]]^2 == hbS^2/4}, {lamS > 0, mS > 0, hbS > 0}]
```

Both hold with every constant symbolic. Second, the whole road, not just its end: this flow is not merely integrable, it is solvable. Collect the covariances into the $2\times2$ matrix $V$, with $\Sigma_x$ and $\Sigma_p$ on the diagonal and $C$ off it, and the flow is a matrix Riccati equation, $\dot V=AV+VA^{T}+D-4\lambda\,VEV$ with $A$ the Hamilton drift, $D$ the backaction heating, and $E$ picking out the monitored entry. A Riccati flow linearizes by the oldest trick in filtering: write $V=XY^{-1}$ and the pair $(X,Y)$ obeys a linear system whose propagator is one matrix exponential, so

$$
V(t)\;=\;\bigl(M_{11}V_0+M_{12}\bigr)\bigl(M_{21}V_0+M_{22}\bigr)^{-1},\qquad
M=\exp\left[t\begin{pmatrix}A&D\\4\lambda E&-A^{T}\end{pmatrix}\right].
$$

Encode that closed form once, for any strength, trap frequency, and starting covariance:

```wl
ClearAll[covFlow];
covFlow[lam_, w_, {sx0_, c0_, sp0_}] :=
  With[{aM = {{0, 1/mass}, {-mass w^2, 0}}, dM = {{0, 0}, {0, lam hbar^2}},
    eM = {{1, 0}, {0, 0}}, v0 = {{sx0, c0}, {c0, sp0}}},
   Function[t, With[{m4 = MatrixExp[
        ArrayFlatten[{{aM, dM}, {4 lam eM, -Transpose[aM]}}] t]},
     With[{v = (m4[[1 ;; 2, 1 ;; 2]] . v0 + m4[[1 ;; 2, 3 ;; 4]]) .
         Inverse[m4[[3 ;; 4, 1 ;; 2]] . v0 + m4[[3 ;; 4, 3 ;; 4]]]},
      {v[[1, 1]], v[[1, 2]], v[[2, 2]]}]]]];
```

The closed form must own its credential, and the cheap way to certify a solution formula is not to chase its transcendental limits but to differentiate it. At $t=0$ the pair starts at $X=V_0$, $Y=\mathbb{1}$, so the construction's derivative there is $X'(0)-V_0\,Y'(0)$ with both derivatives read straight off the linear system; that combination must equal the Riccati rate at $V_0$. Verify it as a polynomial matrix identity, the trap frequency and all three starting covariances fully symbolic:

```wl
ClearAll[sx0S, c0S, sp0S, wS];
With[{aM = {{0, 1/mS}, {-mS wS^2, 0}}, dM = {{0, 0}, {0, lamS hbS^2}},
  eM = {{1, 0}, {0, 0}}, v0 = {{sx0S, c0S}, {c0S, sp0S}}},
 Simplify[(aM . v0 + dM) - v0 . (4 lamS eM . v0 - Transpose[aM]) ==
   aM . v0 + v0 . Transpose[aM] + dM - 4 lamS v0 . eM . v0]]
```

The identity holds for an arbitrary symmetric starting covariance, and that arbitrariness is what makes the local check global: the formula reproduces the Riccati rate from every starting point, so by uniqueness of the flow's solutions it is the solution, at all times, for any trap. Its long-time destination should then be the steady triple derived above; confirm that numerically at the fixture strength, late enough that the transient is long dead:

```wl
Max[Abs[covFlow[1., 0., {4., 0., 1/16.}][30.] -
   (steadyF /. {lamS -> 1., mS -> 1., hbS -> 1.})]]
```

The flow lands on the derived triple. One limit more, because the steady algebra makes it free: let $\hbar\to0$ and the steady conditional spread collapses entirely,

```wl
Limit[steadyF, hbS -> 0]
```

so in the classical limit continuous measurement pins the particle exactly and the quantum filter becomes its classical Kalman ancestor: the residual width at finite $\hbar$ is quantum mechanics refusing to be pinned. Now bind the exact flow at the fixture parameters of Part III's dispersion check ($\lambda=1$, starting width $2$, so $\Sigma_x(0)=4$ and $\Sigma_p(0)=1/16$ for a pure Gaussian):

```wl
ricF = covFlow[1., 0., {4., 0., 1/16.}];
```

The referee is exact, so whatever daylight appears next belongs to the grid alone. Now run the monitored particle itself, the same seed-7 noise path as the dispersion check but with the measurement on, and overlay the trajectory's conditional width on the Riccati curve:

```wl
solM = smoothNoiseTrajectory[gF, flat, 1., packet[0., 0., 2.], 2., wienerPath[2., 0.005, 7]];
Plot[{Sqrt[xVar[gF, conditional[gF, solM, tt]]], Sqrt[ricF[tt][[1]]]},
 {tt, 0, 2}, PlotStyle -> {Automatic, Dashed}, Frame -> True, GridLines -> Automatic,
 PlotLegends -> Placed[{"conditional width from the grid", "deterministic Riccati flow"}, Below],
 FrameLabel -> {"t", "width"}, PlotLabel -> "one noisy trajectory rides the noise-free width curve"]
```

The two curves lie on top of each other across the whole window: a state kicked by noise at every instant carries a width that never feels the noise, which is the Gaussian closure doing exactly what the algebra promised. Put a number on the overlay, the worst gap along the window:

```wl
Max[Table[Abs[Sqrt[xVar[gF, conditional[gF, solM, tt]]] - Sqrt[ricF[tt][[1]]]],
  {tt, Subdivide[0., 2., 80]}]]
```

The gap is thousands of times smaller than the width change it tracks: the smooth-noise route is integrating the measurement backaction, not merely surviving it.

The width is one number; the conditional state carries a stronger credential, and the steady-state algebra above already named it. Under efficient monitoring a pure state stays pure, and for a Gaussian, purity is the single combination $\Sigma_x\Sigma_p-C^2$, pinned at $\hbar^2/4$. That pinning is not a property of the steady state alone: substitute the three Riccati rates into the combination's own time derivative and it closes,

$$\frac{d}{dt}\left(\Sigma_x\Sigma_p-C^2\right)\;=\;4\lambda\,\Sigma_x\left(\frac{\hbar^2}{4}-\bigl(\Sigma_x\Sigma_p-C^2\bigr)\right),$$

so the purity floor is invariant and attracting at the rate $4\lambda\Sigma_x$, whatever the trap does. Verify that closure symbolically, trap frequency included:

```wl
ClearAll[sxD, cD, spD, wD];
FullSimplify[spD (2 cD/mS - 4 lamS sxD^2) +
   sxD (-2 mS wD^2 cD + lamS hbS^2 - 4 lamS cD^2) -
   2 cD (spD/mS - mS wD^2 sxD - 4 lamS sxD cD) -
  4 lamS sxD (hbS^2/4 - (sxD spD - cD^2))]
```

The rate closes identically, with the trap frequency cancelling on its own: the trap turns the state in phase space but never moves it off the purity shell. Now hold the grid to the whole covariance triple at once, not just the width: read $\Sigma_x$, $C$, $\Sigma_p$ off the seed-7 state at the final time, the momentum pieces through the transform pair, and set each beside the Riccati flow's value:

```wl
covTriple = With[{v = conditional[gF, solM, 2.]},
   With[{fv = ft[v], xm = xMean[gF, v]},
    With[{pm = gF["p"] . Abs[fv]^2},
     {xVar[gF, v], Re[Conjugate[v] . (gF["x"] ift[gF["p"] fv])] - xm pm,
      gF["p"]^2 . Abs[fv]^2 - pm^2}]]];
AssociationThread[{"\[CapitalSigma]x", "C", "\[CapitalSigma]p"},
 Abs[covTriple - ricF[2.]]]
```

All three components sit at the same scale as the width alone did, so the backaction heating in $\Sigma_p$ and the cross correlation in $C$ are integrated as faithfully as the narrowing in $\Sigma_x$. And now the purity credential:

```wl
Abs[covTriple[[1]] covTriple[[3]] - covTriple[[2]]^2 - hbar^2/4]
```

The grid state sits on the purity shell several orders more tightly than any single component matches the Riccati flow: the solver's errors slide the covariance triple along the shell rather than off it, which is exactly what the attracting closure above demands of a small perturbation. A wrong diffusion coefficient could not hide here; it would push the triple off the shell at the rate the closure names.

That was the invariant half of the Gaussian closure. The driven half is sharper still. The conditional means follow the noise, but through a specific filter: writing $\nu'(t)=Y'(t)-2\sqrt{\lambda}\,\langle\hat x\rangle$ for the innovation (what remains of the record after subtracting the signal the current estimate predicts), ordinary calculus on the smooth flow gives

$$
\frac{d\langle\hat x\rangle}{dt}=\frac{\langle\hat p\rangle}{m}+2\sqrt{\lambda}\,\Sigma_x\,\nu'(t),\qquad
\frac{d\langle\hat p\rangle}{dt}=-m\omega^2\langle\hat x\rangle+2\sqrt{\lambda}\,C\,\nu'(t),
$$

with the covariance block feeding the gains, and the covariance block is the exact flow we already hold in closed form, so only the two means actually need integrating. Here is the point that makes this an exact test rather than a limit statement: for the smooth noise these moment equations hold at any drawing step, because the closure is algebra, not stochastic calculus. So the wavefunction on its 128-point grid and a driven pair of means riding exact gains, fed by the same interpolated path, must produce the same conditional mean, and the residual between them is pure solver error with no noise-resolution floor underneath. Run both in a harmonic trap from a displaced start, a fresh seed, the small system integrated tightly since it referees, and read the worst disagreement in the mean position:

```wl
lamT = 0.6; sT = 1.2; x0T = 0.5;
wfM = wienerPath[1., 0.005, 13];
solT = smoothNoiseTrajectory[gF, vHarm, lamT, packet[x0T, 0., sT], 1., wfM];
covT = covFlow[lamT, w0, {sT^2, 0., hbar^2/(4 sT^2)}];
ClearAll[covTN]; covTN[tt_?NumericQ, k_Integer] := covT[tt][[k]];
momT = NDSolveValue[{
    mx'[t] == mp[t]/mass + 2 Sqrt[lamT] covTN[t, 1] (wfM'[t] - 2 Sqrt[lamT] mx[t]),
    mp'[t] == -mass w0^2 mx[t] + 2 Sqrt[lamT] covTN[t, 2] (wfM'[t] - 2 Sqrt[lamT] mx[t]),
    mx[0] == x0T, mp[0] == 0.},
   mx, {t, 0, 1.}, MaxStepSize -> 0.005/16, AccuracyGoal -> 10, PrecisionGoal -> 10];
ClearAll[worstMeanGap];
worstMeanGap[sol_] := Max[Table[Abs[xMean[gF, conditional[gF, sol, tt]] - momT[tt]],
    {tt, Subdivide[0., 1., 80]}]];
worstMeanGap[solT]
```

The grid's complex amplitudes against a driven pair of means, and the two integrators agree on the conditional mean to a small fraction of its swing. This is the continuous-space sibling of the qubit essay's closed record identity: an exact pathwise consequence of the equations that any correct integrator must honor at its own precision, with nothing waiting on the white-noise limit. And because the identity is exact, its residual must be bought down by solver effort alone. The dial that matters is the one Part III flagged: how finely the solver chops the jagged forcing $W'(t)$. Hold the grid and the noise fixed, cap the wavefunction solver's steps four times finer through the call's step-cap option, and watch the residual fall:

```wl
worstMeanGap[smoothNoiseTrajectory[gF, vHarm, lamT, packet[x0T, 0., sT], 1., wfM,
   "StepCap" -> 0.005/16]] / worstMeanGap[solT]
```

The residual falls severalfold with nothing changed but the step cap, confirming its reading as integration error and nothing else: the identity itself is exact at this noise resolution, so only the solvers stand between the two curves, with the residual tracking the cap nearly in proportion.

One certification remains, and it is the one that bites: falsifiability. Everything above would deserve no trust if it also passed with the wrong equation. The wrong equation is one coefficient away, the Itô damping $\lambda/2$ fed to a solver that integrates smoothly, and the assembled call's damping multiplier makes it exactly one number in one call, with every other byte of the discretization shared. Run it on the same seed-7 fixture as the width test, and read both drifts' endpoint width against the Riccati reference:

```wl
solWrong = smoothNoiseTrajectory[gF, flat, 1., packet[0., 0., 2.], 2.,
   wienerPath[2., 0.005, 7], "DampingFactor" -> 1/2];
AssociationThread[{"\[Lambda]", "\[Lambda]/2"},
 {Abs[Sqrt[xVar[gF, conditional[gF, solM, 2.]]] - Sqrt[ricF[2.][[1]]]],
  Abs[Sqrt[xVar[gF, conditional[gF, solWrong, 2.]]] - Sqrt[ricF[2.][[1]]]]}]
```

The Itô damping misses the reference by three orders of magnitude more than the Stratonovich damping, a gap no solver tolerance can produce. In other words, the comparison is genuinely able to fail, and the doubled damping is what makes it pass: the factor of two from Part II is load-bearing, and the machinery would have caught us had we dropped it.

There is one more dial to interrogate, and here this essay parts company with its qubit sibling. Two time steps live in the construction: the noise-drawing step, which fixes the physics, and the solver's step cap, which should carry none. For the qubit, relaxing the cap moved nothing; here the forcing wiggles on a 128-point grid's fastest scales, and the cap earns its keep. Rerun the fixture with the cap relaxed from a quarter of the knot spacing to the full knot spacing, and read the width's distance from the Riccati reference:

Abs[Sqrt[xVar[gF, conditional[gF,
      smoothNoiseTrajectory[gF, flat, 1., packet[0., 0., 2.],
       2., wienerPath[2., 0.005, 7], "StepCap" -> 0.005], 2.]]] - Sqrt[ricF[2.][[1]]]]
```

The distance is small but resolvable, well over an order of magnitude above the pinned-cap residual: unlike the qubit case, the adaptive error control alone does not fully resolve this forcing, so the quarter-knot cap is not a ritual here but a working part. And the third dial, the one that owned Part III's noise-off floor, owns almost nothing here: double the spatial grid under the same noise and cap, and read the monitored width's whole-window distance from the exact flow:

With[{g2 = grid[256, 20.]},

The distance barely moves from its 128-point value: the monitored residual belongs to the cap and the drawing step, not to space. All three dials now have measured owners, space holding the noise-off floor, the cap holding a visible slice at fixed noise, and the drawing step holding which stochastic equation is being solved, which is the ensemble statement Part V closes with.

## Part V: The Ensemble: What Needs Weights and What Does Not

A single record shows the filter working; the statistics live across records. Part I proved two ensemble facts symbolically: averages of quantities linear in $|\tilde\psi\rangle\langle\tilde\psi|$ need no likelihood weight, and the weight itself averages to one. Both are now one ensemble away from being watched. Launch the parallel kernels and share the definitions the trajectories need:

```wl
LaunchKernels[];
DistributeDefinitions[hbar, mass, grid, wienerPath, smoothNoiseTrajectory,
  sampleState, norm2, flat, packet];
```

Fix the ensemble study: a free particle at moderate strength from a narrow packet, a short window, and a few hundred trajectories, each read off at the final time as a raw, unnormalized sample vector:

```wl
gE = grid[128, 20.]; lamE = 0.5; tfE = 0.75; ntraj = 400;
ensembleE = ParallelTable[
   sampleState[gE, smoothNoiseTrajectory[gE, flat, lamE, packet[0., 0., 1.],
     tfE, wienerPath[tfE, 0.005, 3000 + k]], tfE], {k, ntraj}];
```

First the alarm bell. Each raw sample carries its likelihood $\||\tilde\psi\rangle\|^2$, and Part I's trace identity says the ensemble mean of that weight is pinned at one for all time; a wrong drift, by contrast, sends it off exponentially, which makes this single number the cheapest wiring check the construction owns. Read the mean weight's distance from one in units of its own standard error:

```wl
weightsE = Total[Abs[ensembleE]^2, {2}];
Abs[Mean[weightsE] - 1]/(StandardDeviation[weightsE]/Sqrt[ntraj])
```

The mean weight sits within ordinary sampling scatter of one, so the drift, the diffusion, and their Stratonovich bookkeeping are consistent across the whole ensemble, not merely on the fixtures of Part IV. The weights are legitimately spread out, though, and their spread measures how unequally the records share the ensemble's statistical work. The standard summary is the ratio $(\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2$, which counts how many equally-weighted trajectories the weighted ensemble is effectively worth (the effective sample size of importance sampling); read it beside the raw count:

```wl
{ntraj, Total[weightsE]^2/Total[weightsE^2]}
```

The weighting spends a real share of the nominal count at this strength and horizon, and what survives is what funds the error bars of every weighted estimate below; push the horizon out and the surviving fraction falls, which is the practical boundary of the reference-measure ensemble, and Part VIII returns to it with a longer run.

Now the Lindblad fact. The unconditional second moments obey the closed chain $\dot\Sigma_x^{u}=2C^u/m$, $\dot C^u=\Sigma_p^u/m$, $\dot\Sigma_p^u=\lambda\hbar^2$ for the free particle: measurement heats the momentum at the constant rate $\lambda\hbar^2$, the parent essay's backaction fact, and two integrations pass the heat into position. Solve the chain exactly, every constant symbolic, so the structure shows itself rather than being asserted:

```wl
ClearAll[uncondSym, lamU, sx0U, sp0U];
uncondSym = FullSimplify[DSolveValue[{sxu'[t] == 2 cu[t]/mS, cu'[t] == spu[t]/mS,
    spu'[t] == lamU hbS^2, sxu[0] == sx0U, cu[0] == 0, spu[0] == sp0U},
   {sxu[t], spu[t]}, t]]
```

There is the whole story in two lines: momentum takes the heat linearly, and position receives it as the cubic term riding on the free ballistic spreading, the momentum random walk integrated twice. Bind the chain at the ensemble's parameters, keeping both numbers for the cells below:

```wl
{uncondSx, uncondSp} = uncondSym /. {mS -> mass, hbS -> hbar, lamU -> lamE,
    sx0U -> 1., sp0U -> hbar^2/4., t -> tfE};
AssociationThread[{"\[CapitalSigma]x", "\[CapitalSigma]p"}, {uncondSx, uncondSp}]
```

Note where the heating's share sits: over this short window it is the majority of the momentum moment but only a sliver of the position one, so the momentum is where a wrong heating rate would stand convicted, while the position mostly certifies the free flow the heat rides on. The grid ensemble must reproduce both numbers through nothing but raw quadratic forms: $\mathrm{Tr}[\hat x^2\rho]$ is linear in $\rho$, so averaging $\langle\tilde\psi|\hat x^2|\tilde\psi\rangle$ with no weights and no normalization must land on it. Read the position moment's standardized gap:

```wl
x2raw = Abs[ensembleE]^2 . gE["x"]^2;
Abs[Mean[x2raw] - uncondSx]/(StandardDeviation[x2raw]/Sqrt[ntraj])
```

The unweighted average of an unnormalized quadratic form lands on the exact Lindblad moment within sampling scatter: the likelihood weight really does live inside the raw state, which is the cleanest statement of why linear observables are weight-free. The sharp half of the claim is the momentum's. Rotate each raw sample to the momentum basis with the transform pair, where the same quadratic form is again a plain dot product, and read that moment's standardized gap:

```wl
p2raw = Abs[ft /@ ensembleE]^2 . gE["p"]^2;
Abs[Mean[p2raw] - uncondSp]/(StandardDeviation[p2raw]/Sqrt[ntraj])
```

The momentum moment, where the backaction heating is the dominant share, lands within sampling scatter too: the ensemble certifies the heating rate itself, not merely the free flow it rides on, and this is the cell that earns the phrase "rebuilds the Lindblad master equation".

Where the weight earns its keep is one nonlinearity away. Ask instead for the spread of the conditional mean position across records, a quantity quadratic in the normalized state. The law of total variance fixes its exact value: the unconditional variance splits into the width every conditional state shares (the deterministic Riccati value) plus the variance of where those conditional states sit,

$$
\mathrm{Var}\bigl(\langle\hat x\rangle\bigr) \;=\; \Sigma_x^{u}(t)\;-\;\Sigma_x(t),
$$

both terms already in hand. Compute the target from the two references, and the conditional means, each raw first moment divided by its own weight:

```wl
ricE = covFlow[lamE, 0., {1., 0., hbar^2/4.}];
targetVar = uncondSx - ricE[tfE][[1]];
mxE = (Abs[ensembleE]^2 . gE["x"])/weightsE;
```

An estimate deserves an error bar, and this estimator's is priced the way every neighboring cell's was, by its own scatter: resample the four hundred trajectories with replacement, recompute the estimate on each resample, and the spread of those recomputations is the estimator's standard error. Define that yardstick once, for any estimator built on trajectory indices, then read the weighted estimate's gap from the target in its own standard errors:

```wl
ClearAll[spreadGapSE, wSpread];
spreadGapSE[est_] := BlockRandom[SeedRandom[77];
   Abs[est[Range[ntraj]] - targetVar]/
    StandardDeviation[Table[est[RandomChoice[Range[ntraj], ntraj]], {800}]]];
wSpread[idx_] := With[{w = weightsE[[idx]]/Mean[weightsE[[idx]]], m = mxE[[idx]]},
   Mean[w m^2] - Mean[w m]^2];
spreadGapSE[wSpread]
```

The weighted estimate lands on the law-of-total-variance target within one of its own standard errors. Now remove the weights and put the same yardstick on the same estimator:

```wl
ClearAll[uSpread];
uSpread[idx_] := With[{m = mxE[[idx]]}, Mean[m^2] - Mean[m]^2];
spreadGapSE[uSpread]
```

The unweighted spread misses the target by tens of its own standard errors: without its likelihood, each record testifies with the wrong voice, and the ensemble votes itself away from the physical measure, decisively. This pair of cells is the measure bookkeeping of Part I made operational: linear observables free, nonlinear conditional statistics weighted, nothing in between.

Last, the promised sweep of the one physics dial. The drawing step of the noise fixes which stochastic equation the smooth forcing represents, so coarsening it should move the ensemble's physics while refining it should not; the Lindblad moment just computed is the natural meter. But comparing two independently drawn ensembles would squander the question in their unshared randomness. Instead, coarsen the very same paths: keep every fifth knot of each trajectory's own Wiener path, reinterpolate, rerun, and read the paired difference of the position moment, trajectory by trajectory, in units of its own standard error, so that everything the two runs share cancels and only the smoothing bias remains:

```wl
ClearAll[coarsen];
coarsen[wf_, k_] := With[{kn = wf["Coordinates"][[1]]},
   Interpolation[Transpose[{kn[[1 ;; ;; k]], wf /@ kn[[1 ;; ;; k]]}],
    InterpolationOrder -> 3]];
coarsePaths = Table[coarsen[wienerPath[tfE, 0.005, 3000 + k], 5], {k, ntraj}];
ensembleC = ParallelTable[
   sampleState[gE, smoothNoiseTrajectory[gE, flat, lamE, packet[0., 0., 1.], tfE,
     coarsePaths[[k]]], tfE], {k, ntraj}];
x2rawC = Abs[ensembleC]^2 . gE["x"]^2;
Abs[Mean[x2rawC - x2raw]]/(StandardDeviation[x2rawC - x2raw]/Sqrt[ntraj])
```

The paired difference sits within ordinary scatter of zero, and the pairing is what gives that verdict its weight: with the shared randomness cancelled, this comparison resolves smoothing bias many times finer than two independent ensembles could, and still finds none at a fivefold coarser draw. At this strength the working step carries real margin, exactly the situation the qubit essay found for its mean; the dial is real, but it is already turned far enough.

## Part VI: Filtering Across Engines: One Record, Two Integrators, One State

Part I called the linear equation a filter: feed it a record, and it produces the conditional state consistent with that record. So far every record we fed it was a plain Wiener path standing in for the raw detector output. The claim has a far stronger form: feed it a record produced by a different integrator built on different mathematics, and it must reconstruct that integrator's conditional state, pathwise, with no ensemble to hide in. The parent essay's engine is exactly such a stranger: it never sees a stochastic differential equation, advancing instead by an exact Bayesian reweighting followed by a split-step Fourier drift. One record, two unrelated machines, one state: that is the test.

Port the parent engine in its two moves, the Gaussian-likelihood measurement update and the symmetric split drift:

```wl
ClearAll[measUpdate, evolveStep, stepV];
measUpdate[g_, lam_, dt_, xb_][psi_] := Normalize[Exp[-lam dt (g["x"] - xb)^2] psi];
evolveStep[g_, vfun_, dt_] := With[{kin = Exp[-I g["p"]^2 dt/(2 mass hbar)],
   pot = Exp[-I vfun[g["x"]] dt/(2 hbar)]},
  Function[psi, pot ift[kin ft[pot psi]]]];
stepV[g_, vfun_, lam_, dt_][psi_] := With[{xb = RandomChoice[Abs[psi]^2 -> g["x"]] +
     RandomVariate[NormalDistribution[0, 1/Sqrt[4 lam dt]]]},
   {evolveStep[g, vfun, dt][measUpdate[g, lam, dt, xb][psi]], xb}];
```

Run the engine in the harmonic trap from the ground state, keeping the conditional state and the outcome at every step; each outcome $\bar x_k$ is the record increment in position units, so the running record is their weighted cumulative sum, $\Delta Y_k=2\sqrt{\lambda}\,\Delta t\,\bar x_k$:

```wl
dtK = 0.01; ntK = 100; lamK = 0.6;
{statesK, outsK} = BlockRandom[SeedRandom[21];
   Transpose[Rest[FoldList[stepV[gF, vHarm, lamK, dtK][First[#]] &,
      {gaussian[gF, 0., 0., Sqrt[hbar/(2 mass w0)]], 0.}, Range[ntK]]]]];
recordY = Interpolation[Transpose[{dtK Range[0, ntK],
     Prepend[Accumulate[2 Sqrt[lamK] dtK outsK], 0.]}], InterpolationOrder -> 3];
```

Now the filtering step, and notice that nothing about `smoothNoiseTrajectory` changes: the record path enters exactly where the Wiener path did, because the linear equation never knew the difference; that indifference is the filter property. The one bookkeeping point is the smoothing correction: the record's fluctuating part is the same Wiener noise, so its quadratic variation is the same, and the doubled damping of Part II carries over unchanged. Drive the equation with the interpolated record from the same initial state:

```wl
solK = smoothNoiseTrajectory[gF, vHarm, lamK,
   packet[0., 0., Sqrt[hbar/(2 mass w0)]], dtK ntK, recordY];
```

The two engines now hold rival conditional states at every step. Compare them where a state comparison is sharpest, the overlap: read the worst infidelity, one minus the squared overlap between the normalized grid samples, over the full window:

```wl
Max[MapThread[1 - Abs[Conjugate[conditional[gF, solK, #1]] . #2]^2 &,
  {dtK Range[ntK], statesK}]]
```

The infidelity stays small along the whole record: two integrators that share not one line of numerics, one advancing by Bayes' rule and Fourier transforms, the other by a finite-difference method of lines on a smoothed record, agree on the conditional state to high fidelity at every step. What residual remains is the discretization mismatch between a step-wise record and its smooth interpolant, so it must shrink when the record is cut finer, with one proviso Part IV taught us to expect: a finer record has a wilder interpolant, so the filter must also be allowed proportionally finer steps, or its own integration error takes over the comparison. Cut the engine's step five times finer, rebuild the record, refilter with the cap tightened through the call's step-cap option, and read the same worst infidelity:

```wl
{statesK2, outsK2} = BlockRandom[SeedRandom[22];
   Transpose[Rest[FoldList[stepV[gF, vHarm, lamK, 0.002][First[#]] &,
      {gaussian[gF, 0., 0., Sqrt[hbar/(2 mass w0)]], 0.}, Range[500]]]]];
recordY2 = Interpolation[Transpose[{0.002 Range[0, 500],
     Prepend[Accumulate[2 Sqrt[lamK] 0.002 outsK2], 0.]}], InterpolationOrder -> 3];
solK2 = smoothNoiseTrajectory[gF, vHarm, lamK,
   packet[0., 0., Sqrt[hbar/(2 mass w0)]], 1., recordY2, "StepCap" -> 0.002/16];
Max[MapThread[1 - Abs[Conjugate[conditional[gF, solK2, #1]] . #2]^2 &,
  {0.002 Range[500], statesK2}]]
```

The infidelity falls by more than an order of magnitude below even the coarse record's value, confirming the reading: the residual is the conditioning mismatch of a step-wise record against its smooth stand-in, it shrinks with the record's step once the filter is allowed to resolve the finer interpolant, and nowhere in it is there a disagreement about the physics. This closes the loop the two essays share: the parent built the conditional state from Bayes' rule, the qubit companion built state and record from one stochastic equation, and here a record from the first construction drives the second and lands on the same state. Filtering is not a metaphor for what the linear equation does; it is the computation, and two unrelated engines just performed it on the same data.

## Part VII: Leaving the Quadratic Nest: a Double Well and a Cat

Every state so far was a Gaussian in a quadratic potential, which is precisely where closed-form referees exist, and precisely what the construction never assumed. The potential entered `smoothNoiseTrajectory` as one pointwise factor, so trading the trap for a double well costs one definition, and it buys the regime the closing claim is about: a state with two peaks, a potential with two valleys, and no five-moment closure anywhere to check against. What stays on duty are the model-free instruments, the guards, and they are about to be needed for real work.

Fix a quartic with valleys at $x=\pm3$ and start the particle as a cat, one lobe resting in each valley, built from the same packet helper:

```wl
gW = grid[256, 20.]; ClearAll[vWell, catWell];
vWell[x_] := 0.02 (x^2 - 9.)^2;
catWell[x_] := (packet[-3., 0., 0.8][x] + packet[3., 0., 0.8][x])/Sqrt[2];
solW = smoothNoiseTrajectory[gW, vWell, 0.5, catWell, 1.5, wienerPath[1.5, 0.005, 29]];
```

First certify that this really is the regime the closures cannot reach. A Gaussian's fourth central moment is locked to its second (three times its square), so the excess kurtosis, the fourth central moment over the squared second minus that Gaussian three, measures the departure, and a two-lobed density drives it far negative. Read it at the start and at the end of the run:

```wl
ClearAll[excessKurtosis];
excessKurtosis[g_, v_] := With[{p = Abs[v]^2, xm = xMean[g, v]},
   (g["x"] - xm)^4 . p/((g["x"] - xm)^2 . p)^2 - 3];
{excessKurtosis[gW, conditional[gW, solW, 0.]],
 excessKurtosis[gW, conditional[gW, solW, 1.5]]}
```

The start sits far below the Gaussian benchmark, as a two-lobed state must, and the endpoint has climbed back toward it: something destroyed the two-lobed structure along the way. Watch it happen through the probability of finding the particle right of center:

```wl
ClearAll[rightMass];
rightMass[g_, v_] := Total[Pick[Abs[v]^2, UnitStep[g["x"]], 1]];
ListLinePlot[Table[{tt, rightMass[gW, conditional[gW, solW, tt]]},

Handing that linear equation to `NDSolve` takes one change of calculus, the damping doubled from $\lambda\hat x^2/2$ to $\lambda\hat x^2$ because a smooth noise integrates in the Stratonovich sense, and one discretization decision, the tensor-product grid pinned to our own points with periodicity stated as an equation.
