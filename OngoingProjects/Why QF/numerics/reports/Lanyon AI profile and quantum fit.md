# Lanyon proves stencil algebra, not convergence

Lanyon AI, Inc. is a Princeton, New Jersey startup that three computational physicists from the Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory (PPPL), Jonathan Gorard (CEO), Ammar Hakim (CTO) and James Juno (Chief Scientist), brought out of stealth on 17 July 2026, announcing a **$10.6M initial round led by Dimension** a month later. As of 4 October 2026 it names no employees, customers or prices, and no peer-reviewed paper describes its method. Its product is a proprietary agent in which an LLM writes a short Racket specification of a PDE system and deterministic symbolic code expands it into C kernels and Lean 4 proofs for explicit finite-volume and discontinuous Galerkin (DG) solvers of hyperbolic equations: advection, Euler, Maxwell, ideal MHD and Vlasov. The published proofs type-check with no escape hatches, but they certify **local algebraic identities over the real numbers about spatial building blocks**. The time integrator, the driver, floating point, stability, convergence and the correctness of the specification all lie outside them, and the advertised hyperbolicity theorems are tautologies. That scope is why the method fits quantum simulation numerics poorly. In Schrödinger and Lindblad solvers, Fock truncation and tensor-network contraction, the documented failures are adaptive steps that skip pulses, cutoffs that converge slowly or to the wrong limit, single-precision floors on infidelity, invariants repaired cosmetically and bond dimensions never checked for convergence. These are global, analytic or floating-point properties, and Lanyon's published Lean states no theorem of that kind for any equation. What transfers is the architecture, in which one specification emits both code and proof obligations and nothing is emitted when they fail, and a template of exact identities whose quantum analogues (Hermiticity, trace annihilation, Kraus completeness, the truncated commutator defect) say more than the classical originals. A credible quantum version would have to prove computable error bounds for time stepping and truncation, with explicit assumptions about the infinite-dimensional limit, carry them through floating point, and tie them to the code that runs. For the Wolfram Quantum Framework (QF), which hands integration to `NDSolveValue`, the feasible form is a checked certificate for each run rather than generated proofs.

## One Racket specification emits C kernels and Lean lemmas

Lanyon generates new solvers; nothing public suggests it can audit existing Fortran, C++ or Python code. A user types terse slash commands such as "/specify Create a 1D advection-diffusion solver", without choosing a scheme or limiter, and an undisclosed LLM writes a fragment of a proprietary Racket DSL that declares the state, fluxes and wave speeds ([Advection-Diffusion note](https://lanyon.ai/research/advection-diffusion/); [maxwell_1d.rkt](https://github.com/lanyonai/MaxwellEquations/blob/main/specifications/maxwell_1d.rkt)). In the company's words, "purely symbolic algorithms expand that specification into code and proofs simultaneously. If the specification cannot be rigorously proven to be correct, the code simply doesn't generate" ([Announcing Lanyon AI](https://lanyon.ai/blog/welcome/)). A `/verify` step gates `/compile`, and `/simulate` then writes a driver from a prose description, using a retrieval pipeline that can pull initial conditions from a cited web page, and runs it in a container. The outputs are Lean 4 files over Mathlib's real numbers and double-precision C kernels. The computer algebra behind the research notes is Maxima, inherited from Gkeyll, and no component of the stack is Wolfram technology ([LanyonScripts](https://github.com/lanyonai/LanyonScripts)). Every public demonstration is an explicit finite-volume or modal DG scheme on a structured grid for a first-order hyperbolic or advection-diffusion system: advection-diffusion, Burgers, isothermal and full Euler, Maxwell, Maxwell on a Kerr background, ideal MHD with and without divergence cleaning, and electrostatic Vlasov in two to six phase-space dimensions ([github.com/lanyonai](https://github.com/lanyonai)). The schemes are standard ones: Lax-Friedrichs and Rusanov fluxes, minmod reconstruction, modal DG with recovery-based diffusion and Dedner divergence cleaning ([Ideal MHD note](https://lanyon.ai/research/ideal-mhd/)). Generation is fast. IdealMHD's 49,317 Lean lines and 31,964 C lines took about 434 seconds to produce, and the Lean takes just under four minutes to type-check ([IdealMHD](https://github.com/lanyonai/IdealMHD); [Ideal MHD note](https://lanyon.ai/research/ideal-mhd/)).

The method continues two preprints written at PPPL. "Shock with Confidence" (Gorard and Hakim, March 2025) built a Racket pipeline that generates C for hyperbolic solvers together with proofs of "L^2 stability, flux conservation, and physical validity", using a theorem-proving framework said to respect "the algebraic structure of floating-point arithmetic" ([arXiv:2503.13877](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13877)). Its body is more careful than its abstract. IEEE-safe rewriting was applied "wherever possible" using rules the authors call "somewhat complex and ad hoc"; each proof is executed Racket code rather than a Lean or Coq term; and the proofs for the superbee and van Leer limiters and for isothermal Euler were partial or conditional ([arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.13877)). That pipeline is open source in Gkeyll's `gkylcas` repository, and Gkeyll itself holds executable checks such as a CFL-stability proof for a Lax-Friedrichs Burgers scheme ([gkylcas](https://github.com/gkeyllorg/gkylcas); [gkeyll](https://github.com/gkeyllorg/gkeyll)). BEACONS (Gorard, Hakim and Juno, February 2026) extended the approach to neural-network PDE solvers with worst-case L∞ error bounds ([arXiv:2602.14853](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14853)). Neither preprint lists a journal reference, Gorard has posted nothing to arXiv since BEACONS, and no paper carries a Lanyon affiliation ([arXiv query](https://export.arxiv.org/api/query?search_query=au:Gorard&sortBy=submittedDate&sortOrder=descending&max_results=45)). The founders' other joint work is in numerical relativity and inverse problems for fusion and astrophysics ([arXiv:2510.26019](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.26019); [arXiv:2505.03849](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03849)). The Lanyon-era record is self-published: nine research notes by Hakim, Gorard and Juno, with a tenth on the Godunov-Peshkov-Romenskii continuum model "in preparation"; four blog posts; and seven solver repositories of generated output that contain no generator, build configuration or drivers ([Research notes](https://lanyon.ai/research/)). A promised note on "the internal operation of Lanyon's symbolic theorem-prover" had not appeared by 4 October 2026 ([Linear PDE benchmarking](https://lanyon.ai/research/linear-benchmarking/)).

What the Lean files assert is narrower than their size suggests. Every finite-volume file instantiates the same thirteen theorem kinds per direction, which is why four repositories report exactly 156 theorems: hyperbolicity, wave stability, consistency of diffusive fluxes, waves, fluctuations and reconstructions, a wave jump condition, flux conservation, linearity preservation and reconstruction symmetry ([AdvectionDiffusion proofs](https://github.com/lanyonai/AdvectionDiffusion/tree/main/proofs); [IdealMHD proofs](https://github.com/lanyonai/IdealMHD/tree/main/proofs)). The substantive ones are per-interface identities: left and right fluctuations sum to the flux jump, equal neighbouring states reconstruct to themselves, linear data are reconstructed exactly, minmod is symmetric. **No published theorem states TVD, L² stability of the update, positivity, a CFL condition, convergence or order of accuracy.** The hyperbolicity theorem is a tautology. In the 1D advection file it reads `∃ r1 : Real, r1 = (xFluxJacobianEigenExprs C P U).lambda1` and is proved by `rfl`, and no file relates the eigenvalue expressions to the flux Jacobian; a re-read for this report confirmed the statement verbatim ([linear_advection_1d.lean#L100](https://github.com/lanyonai/AdvectionDiffusion/blob/main/proofs/linear_advection_1d.lean#L100)). In the ideal-MHD files the eigenvalue expressions use `Real.sqrt`, which Mathlib defines as zero for negative arguments, so "real eigenvalues" holds by typing even where the true eigenvalues would be complex. The research notes link exactly these lines as their hyperbolicity proofs ([hyperbolic_ideal_mhd_3d.lean#L1417](https://github.com/lanyonai/IdealMHD/blob/main/proofs/hyperbolic_ideal_mhd_3d.lean#L1417); [Ideal MHD note](https://lanyon.ai/research/ideal-mhd/)).

Three further gaps separate the proofs from the phrase "formally verified solver". First, the proofs concern real numbers: no published Lean file uses a floating-point type, so the stated IEEE-754 discipline lives only in the proprietary Racket rewriting ([Linear PDE benchmarking](https://lanyon.ai/research/linear-benchmarking/)). Second, the C carries runtime checks with a 10⁻⁸ tolerance where the Lean states exact equalities, and **no artifact links the C to the Lean**: there is no refinement proof, verified extraction or Frama-C annotation ([linear_advection_1d.c](https://github.com/lanyonai/AdvectionDiffusion/blob/main/implementations/linear_advection_1d.c)). Third, each of the 72 published C files ends in an empty `main` whose body is `// Insert simulation drivers here.`, so the time integrator, boundary conditions and driver that `/simulate` writes are neither published nor verified. The generator itself, meaning the DSL compiler, the Lisp prover and both emitters, is proprietary and unverified, so it sits in the trusted computing base. The misformalization risk Lanyon attributes to LLM autoformalization has therefore moved into the template rather than disappeared, and the hyperbolicity theorem is that risk realized. Whether the 2025 Racket checks for CFL stability and TVD still run inside Lanyon before Lean emission is not stated. The artifacts are, however, clean. An independent compile of two files with Lean v4.30.0 and a May 2026 Mathlib finished without errors, `#print axioms` listed only the three standard axioms, and none of the 72 Lean files contains `sorry`, `admit`, `native_decide` or an `axiom` declaration, although the repositories do not pin Lean or Mathlib versions ([lanyonai repositories](https://github.com/lanyonai)).

## The company's own footnotes narrow its headline claims

Lanyon's launch post says "it is mathematically impossible for Lanyon ever to make a mistake" and that Lanyon is "100% reliable". A footnote in the same post narrows this to "mathematically impossible for Lanyon ever to commit a misformalization … Lanyon guarantees perfect syntactic correctness. The issue of semantic correctness … remains an open area of research for us" ([Announcing Lanyon AI](https://lanyon.ai/blog/welcome/)). The August press release repeats the strong form without the footnote ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/lanyon-ai-emerges-from-stealth-to-build-the-future-of-scientific-and-technical-computing-302852383.html)). The technical notes concede more. "The /verify step does not check if the equation or proofs of correctness are semantically correct"; that job falls to a retrieval pipeline that is "not 100% foolproof" ([Advection-Diffusion note](https://lanyon.ai/research/advection-diffusion/)). The DG Burgers limiters "are not verified", so total-variation boundedness cannot be proved and single-cell oscillations appear, and Euler solutions satisfy TVD only "heuristically" ([Burgers note](https://lanyon.ai/research/burgers-equation/); [Nonlinear PDE benchmarking](https://lanyon.ai/research/nonlinear-benchmarking/)). The two benchmark notes measure something narrower than the press release implies. Juno ran five frontier models (Kimi K3, Claude Opus 4.8, Claude Fable 5, GPT-5.5 and GPT-5.6 Sol) three times each on 1D advection, a 2D Maxwell plane wave, Burgers and a 2D Euler Riemann problem, with detailed and terse prompts, and had **the same five models grade one another's anonymized output** as faithful, partial, misformalized or disconnected ([Linear PDE benchmarking](https://lanyon.ai/research/linear-benchmarking/); [Nonlinear PDE benchmarking](https://lanyon.ai/research/nonlinear-benchmarking/)). Lanyon's own output was not graded by the panel. It appears as single reference figures, such as about 7 seconds and 800 output tokens for 1D advection against 18k to 153k tokens for the frontier models, together with an assertion that only Fable 5, with the detailed prompt, "came even close to Lanyon in terms of accuracy" on Euler. With detailed prompts all five models were rated faithful in three trials out of three on advection, Maxwell and Burgers; failures concentrated in terse prompts and in the 2D Euler C code. Lanyon notes that its own figure "is inflated once you include the cost of writing the simulation driver". The result supports a claim about token counts. It does not show that Lanyon's solvers are more accurate than frontier-model solvers, and nobody has replicated it.

Measured against the verification and validation (V&V) practice its target buyers follow, Lanyon offers a formal, partial form of **code verification** (consistency and conservation of discrete operators) and nothing for **solution verification** (error estimates for a given run) or **validation** (agreement with experiment). None of its 17 web pages or two preprints mentions ASME V&V 10, 20 or 40, NAFEMS, DO-178C, NQA-1, manufactured solutions, Richardson extrapolation or grid convergence, and its comparisons with exact solutions are visual: "The exact and Lanyon-computed solutions are essentially identical" ([Burgers note](https://lanyon.ai/research/burgers-equation/)). The FDA's 2023 credibility guidance points to ASME V&V 40, nuclear codes fall under NQA-1 software quality assurance and commercial-grade dedication, and airborne tools fall under DO-178C and its DO-330 tool-qualification supplement ([FDA guidance](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/assessing-credibility-computational-modeling-and-simulation-medical-device-submissions); [ASME VVUQ portfolio](https://www.asme.org/getmedia/c9d712f3-cc5b-4326-b6e4-bd53c50ed9a6/ASME-VVUQ-Standards-Portfolio-Brochure-3-2026_FINAL.pdf); [EFCOG/NRC dedication guide](https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ml1217/ML12171A417.pdf); [NASA formal-methods tool qualification](https://shemesh.larc.nasa.gov/fm/FMinCert/NASA-CR-2017-219371.pdf)). These frameworks expect code verification, typically by manufactured solutions and order of accuracy, solution verification by grid convergence, and validation. None of them requires formal proof of simulation code, and nothing found shows the FDA or NRC crediting it, so Lanyon would have to argue that its proofs map onto their credibility factors. The precedent for what "end-to-end" means in numerics is Boldo and colleagues' 2013 proof of a C program for the 1D wave equation, which bounds both the method error and the round-off error, with obligations generated by Frama-C and discharged in Coq and Gappa ([arXiv:1112.1795](https://arxiv.org/abs/1112.1795)).

No other company makes Lanyon's specific claim. The better-funded formal-verification AI companies target mathematics and general software: Axiom Math raised $200M at a $1.6B valuation in March 2026 to verify AI-generated code with Lean, Harmonic raised $120M at $1.45B in November 2025, and Theorem raised a $6M seed for systems code ([Dealroom](https://dealroom.co/news/126771-axiom-math-raises-200m-to-verify-ai-generated-code-with-mathematics/); [Implicator](https://www.implicator.ai/ai-cracked-research-math-harmonic-just-priced-the-consequence-at-1-45-billion/); [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/security/theorem-wants-to-stop-ai-written-bugs-before-they-ship-and-just-raised-usd6m)). Sound static analysers such as AbsInt's Astrée prove the absence of floating-point exceptions in avionics C, not discretization accuracy ([AbsInt](https://www.absint.com/astree/index.htm)). Lanyon does not appear in the competitor lists found for either the formal-verification labs or the AI-for-simulation companies. None of this suggests a front. The founders are established computational physicists, the company's existence is corroborated by a state registry entry and an independent donor list, its technical work by a substantial public repository record, and the research lineage matches the product. The risk is the distance between "provably correct simulation" and what the proofs state, and the company's own footnotes admit that distance.

## Quantum solvers fail where Lanyon's guarantee stops

Lanyon has said nothing technical about quantum computing, quantum simulation, the Wolfram Language or Mathematica. A full-text search of its 17 web pages finds quantum mechanics and Wolfram only in historical asides in the September essay, and the press release adds one biographical line about the Wolfram Physics Project. Every published template is real-valued, every equation is a classical hyperbolic or advection-diffusion system, and the announced next step, "a provably L² stable, energy conserving discontinuous Galerkin discretization of the Vlasov-Maxwell system", stays classical ([Our Vision](https://lanyon.ai/blog/vision/); [Lanyon blog: Dorland](https://lanyon.ai/blog/dorland/)). The fit question is therefore hypothetical: if the published method were pointed at quantum dynamics, which part would it verify, and is that where quantum numerics fails? The guarantee covers the algebra of the spatial right-hand side and excludes the time integrator, floating point, every convergence statement and the adequacy of the specification. In quantum numerics that split is unfavourable, because the right-hand side is the simplest part and nearly every documented failure lies in the excluded parts.

In QF the whole Lindblad right-hand side, −i[H, ρ] + Σ(LρL† − ½{L†L, ρ}), is one expression built from operator matrices. `QuantumEvolve` hands it to `NDSolveValue` for numeric time ranges, to `DSolveValue` for symbolic ones, or to `MatrixExp` for a time-independent phase-space generator ([QuantumEvolution.m#L93-L97](https://github.com/WolframResearch/QuantumFramework/blob/ed7f7c1ace71b6d1216a32d1e6dcf747d2a267fd/QuantumFramework/Kernel/QuantumEvolution.m#L93-L97); [QuantumEvolution.m#L153-L186](https://github.com/WolframResearch/QuantumFramework/blob/ed7f7c1ace71b6d1216a32d1e6dcf747d2a267fd/QuantumFramework/Kernel/QuantumEvolution.m#L153-L186)). There is no flux, limiter or reconstruction for a template to reason about. The integrator is the solver, and the integrator is where the failures are documented. QuTiP's adaptive integrators skip short pulses unless the user caps the step, so a π/2-wait-π/2 sequence returns an excited population of 0.5 instead of 1 once the wait exceeds about 23 time units ([QuTiP #2814](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/2814); [QuTiP #1265](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/1265)). Its ZVODE path returned a wrong π-wait-π result while "mesolve … raises no warning", and LSODA got it right ([QuTiP #1038](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/1038)). Among the three most widely used Lindblad methods, only the matrix-exponential update is completely positive and trace preserving, so Runge-Kutta and predictor-corrector steps can lose positivity over long runs ([Riesch and Jirauschek](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.00416)). Libraries then repair invariants cosmetically. QuTiP's `mesolve` normalizes output by default "to hide ODE numerical errors"; that repair itself broke the evolution of a non-unit-trace input, and QuTiP's test of the option checks the renormalization rather than the integrator ([mesolve.py](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/blob/56ed7854177e046124118fdcb6b958d557b65984/qutip/solver/mesolve.py#L110-L111); [QuTiP #2426](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/2426); [test_mesolve.py](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/blob/56ed7854177e046124118fdcb6b958d557b65984/qutip/tests/solver/test_mesolve.py#L268-L291)). Lanyon keeps the time integrator out of its verified artifacts, so a Lanyon-style quantum solver would verify the simplest part and leave every one of these failures outside the guarantee.

Fock truncation needs a convergence theorem, and Lanyon's template contains none. The worst cases cannot even be detected numerically. Fischer, Burgarth and Lonigro proved that truncated Hamiltonians converge to the Friedrichs extension of the operator restricted to finite combinations of basis vectors, an extension that "crucially depends on the choice of basis" and can differ from the intended Hamiltonian, in which case "there is no numerical test that can reveal this failure, unless one has the analytical solution to compare with" ([arXiv:2412.15889](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15889)). Truncation also makes the problem stiff, imposing a CFL-type condition on explicit schemes ([Robin, Rouchon and Sellem](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01712)). Computable bounds on both truncation and time-discretization error for Lindblad dynamics were published in 2026 and permit "fully adaptive simulations", and for states with low local occupation a cutoff growing only polylogarithmically in 1/ε suffices ([Etienney, Robin and Rouchon](https://quantum-journal.org/papers/q-2026-03-16-2031/); [Tong et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.06942)). Yet a search of the QuTiP, QuantumOptics.jl, QuantumToolbox.jl and Dynamiqs source trees found no implementation of computable truncation bounds; QuantumToolbox.jl's adaptive cutoff grows a mode when a tail-population heuristic exceeds a tolerance ([time_evolution_dynamical.jl](https://github.com/qutip/QuantumToolbox.jl/blob/fd3dedb7de5331037afaf7a0e69c9e8225d526c9/src/time_evolution/time_evolution_dynamical.jl#L61-L88)). Users carry the cost: one scqubits user took about eight months to find that a default charge cutoff was too small ([scqubits #274](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits/issues/274)).

QF's own Fock operators show what a template of identities can and cannot settle. At finite size, `DisplacementOperator` and `SqueezeOperator` default to the normal-ordered product of exponentials of the truncated a† and a, and offer `"Ordering" -> "Weak"` for the exponential of the truncated generator ([Operators.m#L78-L100](https://github.com/WolframResearch/QuantumFramework/blob/ed7f7c1ace71b6d1216a32d1e6dcf747d2a267fd/QuantumFramework/Kernel/SecondQuantization/Operators.m#L78-L100); [Operators.m#L142-L173](https://github.com/WolframResearch/QuantumFramework/blob/ed7f7c1ace71b6d1216a32d1e6dcf747d2a267fd/QuantumFramework/Kernel/SecondQuantization/Operators.m#L142-L173)). A check run for this report on the repository version (2.1.1, commit ed7f7c1a), at cutoff 8 with α = 0.9 + 0.4i, found that **the default form reproduces the exact matrix elements of D(α) to 1.7×10⁻¹⁵ but is far from unitary (‖D†D − I‖₂ ≈ 1.0), while the weak form is unitary to 10⁻¹⁵ but misses the exact elements by up to 0.44**. The squeeze operator at ξ = 0.9 + 0.4i showed the same split, with exact entries to 1.1×10⁻¹⁵ in the default form against an entry error of 0.62 in the weak form. QF's default therefore follows the "exact elements, then truncate" semantics of Strawberry Fields rather than the "truncate, then exponentiate" default of QuTiP and QuantumOptics.jl ([PennyLane forum](https://discuss.pennylane.ai/t/fock-backend-truncation/3545); [QuTiP states.py](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/blob/master/qutip/core/states.py); [QuantumOpticsBase fock.jl](https://github.com/qojulia/QuantumOpticsBase.jl/blob/master/src/fock.jl)). Each property is an exact identity of the kind Lanyon's prover discharges. The normal-ordered product is exact because the lowering factor acts first, so no entry inside the cutoff depends on levels above it, and the truncated algebra obeys [a, a†] = I − N|N−1⟩⟨N−1|, which the same check confirmed to 10⁻¹⁵. Neither identity says whether 8, 50 or 500 levels suffice for a given state and evolution. That is a tail bound, and it is the question users get wrong: for the cubic-phase gate, the error of the truncate-then-exponentiate construction "at cutoffs in common use is found to be of the same size as the quantity being computed" ([Cordshooli, arXiv:2609.13077](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13077)).

Tensor-network contraction splits the same way. Exact contraction is multilinear algebra, and the statement that a chosen contraction path returns the same tensor as any other is the kind of identity a template prover handles. By default QF compiles a circuit by exact contraction through `TensorNetworkContract`, with a `"Path"` option for the contraction order ([Properties.m#L149-L162](https://github.com/WolframResearch/QuantumFramework/blob/ed7f7c1ace71b6d1216a32d1e6dcf747d2a267fd/QuantumFramework/Kernel/QuantumCircuitOperator/Properties.m#L149-L162); [TensorNetwork.m#L184-L217](https://github.com/WolframResearch/QuantumFramework/blob/ed7f7c1ace71b6d1216a32d1e6dcf747d2a267fd/QuantumFramework/Kernel/QuantumCircuitOperator/TensorNetwork.m#L184-L217)). Even exact contraction has had backend bugs, such as an open ITensor issue titled "Contracting with DiagBlockSparse can give the wrong answer" ([ITensors.jl #1477](https://github.com/ITensor/ITensors.jl/issues/1477)), but most failures sit in approximate contraction and in floating point. ITensor's documentation states that "there is no automatic or bulletproof check for DMRG convergence" ([ITensorMPS DMRG FAQ](https://github.com/ITensor/ITensorMPS.jl/blob/2a98a9902948f8e07bc52dcf07a8a81a9f3caac5/docs/src/faq/DMRG.md?plain=1#L3-L46)). quimb's TEBD error estimate is a Trotter heuristic that leaves out SVD truncation error ([quimb tebd.py](https://github.com/jcmgray/quimb/blob/8120ab0a3e3b5a98bfb26de72d63d17065a3230a/quimb/tensor/tn1d/tebd.py#L284-L287)). Belief-propagation contraction gave answers that depended on the order of updates, including a wrong Bell-state ⟨ZZ⟩ ([quimb #325](https://github.com/jcmgray/quimb/issues/325)). cuSOLVER's SVD silently returned NaN for rank-deficient rectangular matrices during GPU TDVP ([ITensors.jl #1753](https://github.com/ITensor/ITensors.jl/issues/1753)), and low-precision contraction is limited by exponent range rather than mantissa ([Ootomo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08989)). At the research frontier, classical methods disagreed by up to 20% on IBM's 127-qubit kicked-Ising circuits until later work converged them to within 0.01 ([Anand et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17839); [Begušić, Gray and Chan](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05077)). A bound on the weight discarded by one SVD truncation is provable, and so in principle is a composition of such bounds along a sweep, but no method, formal or otherwise, offers a guarantee for TDVP projection error or for belief propagation on loopy graphs. QF's experimental `QuantumMPS`, which keeps the m largest terms of a decomposition, would need the same discarded-weight accounting ([Experimental.m#L87-L88](https://github.com/WolframResearch/QuantumFramework/blob/ed7f7c1ace71b6d1216a32d1e6dcf747d2a267fd/QuantumFramework/Kernel/Experimental.m#L87-L88)).

Floating point and spectral questions widen the gap. Float64 reports a quartic double-well splitting of about 10⁻¹³ against a true value near 10⁻²³ ([QuantumToolbox.jl precision guide](https://qutip.org/QuantumToolbox.jl/stable/users_guide/arbitrary_precision)). Dynamiqs and the `nvidia` target of CUDA-Q default to single precision ([Dynamiqs sharp bits](https://www.dynamiqs.org/stable/documentation/getting_started/sharp-bits.html); [CUDA-Q](https://nvidia.github.io/cuda-quantum/latest/using/backends/sims/svsims.html)). In a local NumPy check done for the underlying research, the naive infidelity 1 − |⟨a|b⟩|² computed in complex64 returned exactly zero for a true infidelity of 10⁻⁷, while the residual form ‖b − ⟨a|b⟩a‖² recovered it. Cirq's density-matrix trace decays toward zero under repeated channels ([Cirq #5916](https://github.com/quantumlib/Cirq/issues/5916)). Proofs over ℝ say nothing about any of this. Spectral claims are the other gap. Steady-state uniqueness is a kernel dimension, and in boundary-dissipated systems the relaxation time is set "not by the gap or low-lying eigenvalues of the Liouvillian but by superexponentially large expansion coefficients" ([Mori and Shirai](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10953)). Solvers fail here without warning: in an open 2026 issue QuTiP's eigenvalue steady-state method "silently returns a completely wrong, non-physical state", and its sparse eigensolver misses eigenvalues of a purely imaginary spectrum ([QuTiP #3026](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/3026); [QuTiP #1998](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/1998)). The one spectral property Lanyon advertises is the one its published proofs state vacuously, and its template never uses Mathlib's determinant, characteristic polynomial or matrix-vector product.

The semantic gap Lanyon leaves to retrieval is where quantum modelling errors live. An open QuTiP issue disputes the definition of the Lindblad dissipator itself, another a spectral-density convention used by two modules, and a reported "wrong phase" in Qiskit Aer was a global-phase convention ([QuTiP #1719](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/1719); [QuTiP #2828](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/2828); [Qiskit Aer #977](https://github.com/Qiskit/qiskit-aer/issues/977)). The rotating-wave and Markov approximations are validation questions that no proof about an implementation can settle. The published-result failures on record were caught by outsiders recomputing: Nature retracted the 2016 Quantum Moves paper in 2020 over an error in its code that other researchers found, and independent groups resolved the 20% spread on IBM's utility circuits ([Nature retraction note](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-2515-2); [Sels, arXiv:1709.08766](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.08766); [Begušić, Gray and Chan](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05077)). The table maps each numerics task to the coverage Lanyon's published method would give it.

| Task | Documented failure | Covered by Lanyon's published method? | What would carry over |
|---|---|---|---|

## A credible quantum version must prove bounds, not identities

Three parts of Lanyon's design are worth adopting. The first is the refusal gate. Quantum libraries do the opposite and repair silently: QuTiP renormalizes by default, Dynamiqs evolves only the Hermitian part of ρ by default, and QuTiP's cached Hermiticity flag reported `True` for non-Hermitian output ([QuTiP mesolve.py](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/blob/56ed7854177e046124118fdcb6b958d557b65984/qutip/solver/mesolve.py#L110-L111); [Dynamiqs mesolve.py](https://github.com/dynamiqs/dynamiqs/blob/58a6baa385f6824fe8ac107b9b26935f19ee6bc6/dynamiqs/integrators/apis/mesolve.py#L188-L191); [QuTiP #2410](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/2410)). Each of these makes the cheapest invariant check vacuous. The second is an independent checker. Anyone can re-run Lanyon's proofs through an off-the-shelf Lean kernel, the arrangement the AI-assisted verification literature finds credible, since LLM-written tests tend to capture "the actual program behaviour rather than the expected one" ([Konstantinou et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.21136)). The same literature locates the weak point exactly where Lanyon's tautologies sit: in an expert review of a sorry-free Lean formalization, agents "adapted well to local, mechanically checkable feedback, but remained weak at choosing definitions" ([Ilin and Nugent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13925)). The third is the template, which states more in quantum than it does for hyperbolic PDEs, because the structural properties that matter are algebraic. Hermiticity of H(t) over a whole drive, Tr L(ρ) = 0 and L(ρ)† = L(ρ†) for a Lindblad generator, Kraus completeness ΣK†K = I for a one-step update, [H, N] = 0 wherever number conservation is assumed, and the truncated commutator defect can each be proved once, symbolically in the dimension. Each would catch the wrong-formula bugs that a study of 223 bugs across 18 quantum platforms lists among its recurring patterns ([Paltenghi and Pradel](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14560); [OOPSLA 2022](https://www.software-lab.org/publications/oopsla2022_quantum.pdf)).

Those identities are the cheap layer. A credible quantum version would also have to prove the statements below, each aimed at a documented failure and each with a starting point in the literature.

| What must be proved | Failure it closes | Starting point |
|---|---|---|
| The discrete update is completely positive and trace preserving, or its positivity loss is bounded | Positivity loss in Runge-Kutta and predictor-corrector steps | Exponential and Kraus-form schemes ([Riesch and Jirauschek](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.00416); [Appelö and Cheng](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08898); [Robin, Rouchon and Sellem](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01712)) |
| A computable time-discretization error estimate is sound, and every declared drive breakpoint was resolved | Pulse skipping; silent step-control failures | Defect-based estimators for splitting methods ([Auzinger, Koch and Thalhammer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11075-014-9935-8)); global error estimates for Magnus-Lanczos propagators ([Kormann thesis](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:549981/FULLTEXT01.pdf)); [Etienney, Robin and Rouchon](https://quantum-journal.org/papers/q-2026-03-16-2031/) |
| A computable truncation bound holds, with the infinite-dimensional generator and the self-adjoint extension it converges to stated as hypotheses | Slow or wrong-limit Fock convergence | [Etienney, Robin and Rouchon](https://quantum-journal.org/papers/q-2026-03-16-2031/); [Tong et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.06942); [Woods, Cramer and Plenio](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.01531); [Fischer, Burgarth and Lonigro](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15889) for the hypotheses |
| Reported numbers carry IEEE-level error bounds or validated enclosures, computed from cancellation-free formulas | Precision floors; cancellation in 1 − F | [VCFloat2](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~appel/papers/vcfloat2.pdf); [FloatLib for Lean](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.19352); interval enclosures of matrix exponentials ([Miyajima](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10444-018-9609-5)); Krylov bounds rigorous up to round-off ([TimeEvolver](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15346)) |
| Spectral outputs come with residual and conditioning bounds, and kernel dimension is certified | Silent wrong steady states; non-normal relaxation | Multi-oracle eigensolvers with arbitrary-precision references ([CERTIFY-ED](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11787)) |
| Approximate contractions account for discarded weight and compose it into a global bound; methods without a bound are flagged | Unchecked bond-dimension convergence; order-dependent belief propagation | Discarded weight and energy-variance extrapolation ([Hubig, Haegeman and Schollwöck](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.01104); [Paeckel et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05824)) |
| The proof refers to the code that runs | Real-number proofs extracted to floating-point code | [Boldo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1112.1795); the [VOQC](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3604630) caveat |

Nothing close to this exists for quantum dynamics. The research found no formally verified Lindblad or many-qubit Schrödinger solver. Formal quantum work covers circuits and compilers; the nearest to simulation is QBlue, a Rocq-verified compiler for second-quantized Hamiltonian simulation whose abstract claims no numerical error bounds ([arXiv:2509.18583](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.18583)). VOQC shows the extraction gap that the last row addresses: its proofs over Coq reals are extracted to OCaml floats, "which may allow floating-point error not accounted for in the proofs" ([VOQC, TOPLAS](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3604630)). The numerical precedents are classical and small: Boldo and colleagues' wave equation; VeriNum's verified leapfrog integrator and Jacobi iteration, with a finite-element method still "in progress"; and Immler's Isabelle-verified ODE solver behind Tucker's Lorenz-attractor proof ([verinum.org](https://verinum.org/); [Immler](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6044317/)). The closest quantum result is Gleipnir, which computes verified error bounds for noisy quantum programs through a diamond norm evaluated with MPS ([arXiv:2104.06349](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06349)). Lanyon's published work covers the identity layer and nothing below it, so a quantum Lanyon would be mostly unbuilt research in formal analysis, not a port of an existing product.

The practical alternative is solution verification with machine-checkable evidence for each run. CERTIFY-ED, a two-author 2026 preprint not yet reviewed, shows the pattern for exact diagonalization: three independent LAPACK paths, thirteen validation layers including arbitrary-precision references and conservation laws, SHA-256-hashed certificates and an error-injection self-test, with agreement against 50-digit `mpmath` to 1.6×10⁻¹⁵ ([arXiv:2605.11787](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11787)). Quantum dynamics suits this approach because it is rich in oracles: norm, trace, Hermiticity, positivity and CPTP conditions; solvable models; trajectory averages that must match the master equation; Loschmidt echoes; and frame changes. Classical manufactured solutions add source terms that break unitarity and complete positivity, but a structure-preserving substitute is easy to construct: choose a unitary U(t) and set H(t) = iU̇U†, so that ψ(t) = U(t)ψ₀ is exact. No published use of that construction was found. Ranked by yield for effort, a harness of this kind would catch step-control failures by sweeping tolerances, step caps and breakpoints; precision artefacts by sweeping precision; invariant violations hidden by repairs; logic bugs through disagreement between methods; truncation and bond-dimension non-convergence through sweeps with tail-weight accounting and, for Lindblad, the Etienney bounds; and trajectory errors through 1/√N scaling. The current bar is low. None of six major libraries' test suites checks positivity of evolved states or uses property-based testing, and pass tolerances of 10⁻³ to 0.6 cannot detect errors at the 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁹ scale relevant to gate fidelities ([Dynamiqs integrator_tester.py](https://github.com/dynamiqs/dynamiqs/blob/58a6baa385f6824fe8ac107b9b26935f19ee6bc6/tests/integrator_tester.py#L16-L45); [QuantumOptics.jl MCWF test](https://github.com/qojulia/QuantumOptics.jl/blob/f9fd606a8cb9bf0cfb821786117253cbdc110911/test/test_timeevolution_mcwf.jl#L164-L177); [QuantumToolbox.jl shifted-Fock test](https://github.com/qutip/QuantumToolbox.jl/blob/fd3dedb7de5331037afaf7a0e69c9e8225d526c9/test/main-test/dynamical-shifted-fock.jl#L71-L72)).

For QF, the certificate route fits the architecture and the proof route does not. QF delegates integration to `NDSolveValue`, closed-form solution to `DSolveValue` and contraction to `TensorNetworkContract`, so a Lanyon-style end-to-end proof would have to formalize Wolfram Language solvers that live outside QF's code. Three features of the current code favour certificates instead. QF's operators are already a machine-readable specification, the object Lanyon has an LLM write, so the identity layer can be checked symbolically before any numerics run. `QuantumEvolve` declares every `NDSolveValue` option and forwards them, so precision, step-cap and method sweeps need no solver changes ([QuantumEvolution.m#L13-L16](https://github.com/WolframResearch/QuantumFramework/blob/ed7f7c1ace71b6d1216a32d1e6dcf747d2a267fd/QuantumFramework/Kernel/QuantumEvolution.m#L13-L16); [QuantumEvolution.m#L160-L166](https://github.com/WolframResearch/QuantumFramework/blob/ed7f7c1ace71b6d1216a32d1e6dcf747d2a267fd/QuantumFramework/Kernel/QuantumEvolution.m#L160-L166)). And `QuantumEvolve` does not renormalize the state. In a check for this report, the non-Hermitian test generator H = −0.5i|1⟩⟨1| left a norm of 0.6065307 at t = 1 against the exact e^(−1/2), and the 2.5×10⁻⁸ relative difference is the integrator's own error showing through. The repair sits one level up, in the properties: `"ProbabilitiesList"` divides the weights by their total, so the same state reports `{0., 1.}`, and for a density matrix that fails `"PhysicalQ"` it reads the diagonal of the eigen-repaired `"Physical"` state ([Properties.m#L295-L299](https://github.com/WolframResearch/QuantumFramework/blob/ed7f7c1ace71b6d1216a32d1e6dcf747d2a267fd/QuantumFramework/Kernel/QuantumState/Properties.m#L293-L297)). Invariant checks on `"Norm"`, `"StateVector"` and `"DensityMatrix"` therefore measure the integrator, which is exactly the signal QuTiP's defaults remove; checks on probabilities do not.

## Conclusion

Lanyon is best read as a well-credentialed research lab less than three months past launch, whose asset is a pipeline that turns a scheme description into matching kernels and a large, re-checkable set of local lemmas in minutes. Getting from there to "provably correct simulation" takes the theorems it has not published: stability, convergence, floating-point error and a link from proof to executable. Its own roadmap names the result that would change this assessment, a "provably L² stable, energy conserving" DG discretization of Vlasov-Maxwell, which would be the first global stability theorem in its public Lean and the first sign that the generator can produce the kind of statement quantum numerics needs ([Lanyon blog: Dorland](https://lanyon.ai/blog/dorland/)). The promised note on its prover, a Form D and the content of Gorard's October interview are the other items to watch. Despite the CEO's history with Wolfram Language quantum functionality, nothing in Lanyon's stack, roadmap or public statements touches quantum or Wolfram technology, so in the near term it is neither a competitor nor a supplier for QF.

For quantum simulation, the comparison reverses the usual ranking of formal methods above testing. The evidence that would most raise trust in a quantum result today is a checked bound on the run itself (truncation, time step and precision), not a proof about the code that produced it, because the failures that change physics conclusions depend on the run and on analysis, and the silent wrong-limit truncation that Fischer and colleagues proved possible can be excluded only by an analytic hypothesis, not by more proofs about code. Investors are paying for correctness claims, from Lanyon's seed to Axiom's and Harmonic's much larger rounds, yet the research found no company selling verification aimed at quantum simulation software. A QF result that ships with stated, checked bounds on cutoff, step and precision would claim less than "formally verified" but would answer the question Lanyon's published proofs leave open: whether the computed number approximates the model's exact solution.
