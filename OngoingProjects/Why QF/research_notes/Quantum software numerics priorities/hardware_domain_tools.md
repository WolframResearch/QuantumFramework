## Q1. scqubits: which problems are featured, and where does it stop (static spectra versus dynamics, time evolution and control)?

### Takeaway
scqubits is the reference tool for *static* superconducting-circuit physics. It covers spectra versus offset charge and flux, charge dispersion, matrix elements, wavefunctions, coupled-circuit dressed spectra, multi-parameter sweeps, formula-based coherence estimates, and arbitrary custom circuits. It does not do time evolution itself; dynamics go through hand-built QuTiP `sesolve`/`mesolve` calls. That QuTiP bridge breaks with QuTiP 5.3.1, which fresh installs received when the bug was filed (Aug 2026; still open in late Sept 2026). There has been no PyPI release since May 2025, although development on `main` is active.

### Cited Findings
**Identity, papers, versions, maintenance**
- Paper: P. Groszkowski and J. Koch, "Scqubits: a Python package for superconducting qubits," *Quantum* 5, 583 (2021), doi:10.22331/q-2021-11-17-583. Built-in qubit classes: transmon, fluxonium, flux qubit, cos(2φ) qubit and 0-π qubit. Features: spectra, plots versus external parameters, matrix elements, wavefunctions, composite Hilbert spaces of qubits coupled to harmonic or weakly anharmonic modes, "an extensive suite of methods for estimating qubit coherence times," GUI widgets, multiprocessing, and "a direct interface to ... QuTiP" for simulating time evolution — [Quantum 5, 583](https://quantum-journal.org/papers/q-2021-11-17-583/)
- Custom-circuit module paper: S. P. Chitta, T. Zhao, Z. Huang, I. Mondragon-Shem, J. Koch, "Computer-aided quantization and numerical analysis of superconducting circuits," *New J. Phys.* 24, 103020 (2022), doi:10.1088/1367-2630/ac94f2. It gives a programmable route from circuit to Hamiltonian for nonlinear circuits — [OSTI record](https://www.osti.gov/pages/biblio/1906056)
- Latest release v4.3.1 (2025-05-17). Earlier: v4.3 (2025-01-09), v4.2 (2024-10-17). PyPI also lists 4.3.1 as the latest version — [GitHub releases](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits/releases), [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/scqubits/)
- `main` is active after that release: at least 100 commits since 2025-06-01 (the API page cap), last push 2026-09-22. Closed PRs opened in 2026 include "Refactor circuit subsystem" (#289) and "Reliable, fast parallel parameter sweeps + live progress" (#307); I did not check whether each was merged. "Visualize branch analysis result" (#282, example with a fluxonium coupled to an oscillator) was merged, with a merge commit dated 2026-06-26. None of this is in a PyPI release as of 2026-10-04. The repo has 283 stars and 25 open issues/PRs — [scqubits repo](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits), [PR #282](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits/pull/282), [PR #289](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits/pull/289), [PR #307](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits/pull/307)

**Featured problems (from the official examples repo, `scqubits-examples/examples/`)**
- The notebooks are: `demo_transmon`, `demo_fluxonium`, `demo_flux_qubit`, `demo_cos2phi_qubit`, `demo_zeropi`, `demo_customcircuit`, `demo_hilbertspace`, `demo_parametersweep`, `demo_noise`, `demo_qutip_fluxoniumcz`, `demo_qutip_fluxoniumreset`, `demo-jaynes-cummings`, `demo_explorer`, `demo_gui` and `scqubits_paper_source_code` — [scqubits-examples](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits-examples/tree/master/examples)
- **Transmon spectrum versus offset charge.** The Hamiltonian is H = 4E_C(n̂−n_g)² − (E_J/2)Σ(|n⟩⟨n+1| + h.c.) in the charge basis. Example parameters are E_J=30.02, E_C=1.2 (GHz), with `plot_evals_vs_paramvals('ng', linspace(-2,2,220), evals_count=6)`. The notebook also covers charge dispersion versus E_J/E_C (`plot_dispersion_vs_paramvals`, transitions 0→1 and 0→2), matrix elements of n̂ and cos φ, and a tunable (SQUID) transmon versus flux — [demo_transmon.ipynb](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits-examples/blob/master/examples/demo_transmon.ipynb)
- **Fluxonium spectrum versus flux.** The Hamiltonian is H = −4E_C∂²_φ − E_J cos(φ−φ_ext) + ½E_Lφ². Example parameters are E_J=8.9, E_C=2.5, E_L=0.5. The notebook plots the spectrum versus flux (with and without ground-state subtraction), wavefunctions and n̂ matrix elements — [demo_fluxonium.ipynb](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits-examples/blob/master/examples/demo_fluxonium.ipynb)
- **Coherence estimates.** Per-channel methods include `tphi_1_over_f_ng()` and `t1_charge_impedance(i=3, j=1, T=0.100)`. Combined estimates come from `t1_effective()` and `t2_effective()`, and `plot_coherence_vs_paramvals` sweeps n_g or flux. Examples cover a charge-regime CPB, a tunable transmon and a fluxonium. The notebook warns that, for the transmon, "`t1_effective()` does not include all the channels that the qubit supports" by default — [demo_noise.ipynb](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits-examples/blob/master/examples/demo_noise.ipynb)
- **Coupled-circuit spectra and sweeps.** `HilbertSpace` couples two transmons to a resonator, and `ParameterSweep` runs a 2D sweep over flux and n_g with dressed-to-bare index lookup — [demo_parametersweep.ipynb](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits-examples/blob/master/examples/demo_parametersweep.ipynb). A second example has two Kerr oscillators coupled through an rf-SQUID, where `bare_index`/`dressed_index` relate dressed states to product states — [demo_hilbertspace.ipynb](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits-examples/blob/master/examples/demo_hilbertspace.ipynb)
- **Custom circuits.** An asymmetric 0-π circuit is specified in YAML (`scq.Circuit(..., ext_basis="discretized")`). The notebook shows the symbolic Lagrangian and Hamiltonian, a variable transformation, hierarchical diagonalization "to reduce computational time," flux sweeps and potential and wavefunction plots — [demo_customcircuit.ipynb](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits-examples/blob/master/examples/demo_customcircuit.ipynb)
- **Driven dynamics are done in QuTiP, written by hand.** The fluxonium CZ tutorial follows Nesterov et al. ([arXiv:1802.03095](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.03095)). It builds drive terms, truncates operators "in two steps" for efficiency, converts GHz to angular frequency by hand, and calls `qt.sesolve`. It notes that with QuTiP 5 the `qip` submodule must be installed separately — [demo_qutip_fluxoniumcz.ipynb](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits-examples/blob/master/examples/demo_qutip_fluxoniumcz.ipynb). The heavy-fluxonium reset tutorial follows Zhang et al., [PRX 11, 011010](https://journals.aps.org/prx/pdf/10.1103/PhysRevX.11.011010). It uses `qt.mesolve` with resonator photon loss √κ a and warns that the simulation cell "will likely require several minutes of computation time" — [demo_qutip_fluxoniumreset.ipynb](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits-examples/blob/master/examples/demo_qutip_fluxoniumreset.ipynb)
- The examples state the design intent: Hamiltonians are returned as `qutip.Qobj` to "facilitate[] simple incorporation of Hamiltonians generated with `scqubits` into any of the solvers QuTiP offers for time evolution" — [demo_hilbertspace.ipynb](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits-examples/blob/master/examples/demo_hilbertspace.ipynb)
- The Circuit class has a `hamiltonian_for_qutip_dynamics` method for time-dependent (parametric) flux driving, documented in a "custom circuit parametric driving" guide. A user reported that a usage change made earlier gate simulations give "nonsensical results" until the DC flux offset was put into the drive function (open issue #258) — [issue #258](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits/issues/258); an earlier bug was "hamiltonian_for_qutip_dynamics Hamiltonian expansion order" (closed, #247) — [issue #247](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits/issues/247)

**Documented limitations and fragility (issue tracker)**
- **QuTiP 5.3.1 breaks `HilbertSpace`.** `Data.as_scipy()` now returns sparse *arrays*, which scqubits' type dispatch does not recognise. "Every `HilbertSpace` workflow crashes; 11 of 19 tests in `scqubits/tests/test_hilbertspace.py` fail on current `main`." Because scqubits requires `qutip>=4.3.1` with no upper bound, "every fresh install gets this combination." The issue is open (filed 2026-08-10), and a related report followed (#327, 2026-09-27) — [issue #320](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits/issues/320), [issue #327](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits/issues/327)
- Other open bugs and requests: `TunableTransmon` lacks `t1_quasiparticle_tunneling()` "despite documentation suggesting so" (#268); `ParameterSweep.energy_by_dressed_index(subtract_ground=True)` "mutates cached evals in place, corrupting subsequent lookups" (#318); an inquiry about shot noise (#280); a request for a tutorial on interfacing scqubits with other packages (#266); performance benchmarking between releases, open since 2023 (#187); and a scipy version cap on macOS (#272) — [open issues](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits/issues)

### Inferences
- scqubits covers static spectra thoroughly: a range of qubit types, custom circuits and sweeps. Pulse-level control is a gap. No official example covers a transmon π-pulse, leakage to |2⟩, or DRAG; the only driven examples are fluxonium CZ and reset, written in QuTiP with manual frames, units and truncation. A worked transmon-dynamics study would therefore not be repeating anything scqubits already offers.
- The coherence module returns T1/Tφ *numbers* from methods that take temperature and level indices, not master-equation trajectories. I infer that the estimates are perturbative and formula-based (golden-rule style). I did not read the noise-model derivations in the docs this session.
- The QuTiP 5.3.1 breakage, together with no release since May 2025, means the documented scqubits→QuTiP dynamics route is currently fragile for new installs.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch the scqubits ReadTheDocs pages for the noise formulas or the full parametric-driving guide. Their existence is attested through issue #258's links.
- I did not confirm whether "branch analysis" (#282) is the readout-resonator dressed-branch or ionization analysis. The fluxonium–oscillator example suggests it, but this is unconfirmed.
- I found no stated scale limits (Hilbert-space sizes or runtimes) in the examples.

## Q2. Pulser and Bloqade: which problems are featured, at what scale, and with which emulation methods?

### Takeaway
Both stacks feature the same physics: Rydberg blockade, adiabatic preparation of Z2/AF order, many-body Rabi enhancement, and MIS/MWIS through the quantum adiabatic algorithm. Bloqade.jl also covers quantum many-body scars, a lattice-gauge-theory mapping and QMC. Exact emulation tops out around 25–27 atoms (state vector, GPU), the blockade subspace stretches that, and MPS/TDVP (emu-mps) is aimed at about 100 atoms. Pulser moved its classic physics tutorials (blockade, AF state, 1D crystals) to `tutorials/retired/` in 2025. Bloqade.jl is in maintenance mode, and QuEra's Python stacks are split between `bloqade-analog`, whose emulation docs are marked "work in progress," and the new gate-focused `bloqade`.

It provides QuTiP-based simulation "for studying and exploring the outcome of pulse sequences for *small systems*" — [Quantum 6, 629](https://quantum-journal.org/papers/q-2022-01-24-629/)

**Pulser: featured problems (details)**
- *Blockade (retired quickstart).* Two atoms receive sequential π-pulses under H = (Ω₁/2)σˣ₁ + (Ω₂/2)σˣ₂ + U n₁n₂, and the separation R is scanned. "The occupation of |rr⟩ is suppressed for decreasing R ... (called the *Rydberg blockade radius*)" — [intro_rydberg_blockade.ipynb](https://github.com/pasqal-io/Pulser/blob/develop/tutorials/retired/intro_rydberg_blockade.ipynb)
- *Blockade radius formula in code:* `rydberg_blockade_radius(Ω) = (interaction_coeff / Ω)**(1/6)` (µm, with Ω in rad/µs) — [_device_datacls.py](https://github.com/pasqal-io/Pulser/blob/develop/pulser-core/pulser/devices/_device_datacls.py). C₆ (and C₃) coefficients for Rydberg levels 50–100 "were calculated using ARC and double checked with PairInteraction" — [interaction_coefficients](https://github.com/pasqal-io/Pulser/blob/develop/pulser-core/pulser/devices/interaction_coefficients/__init__.py)
- *Example (virtual) device limits in the source:* `AnalogDevice` uses Rydberg level 60, at most 80 atoms, a minimum spacing of 5 µm, a maximum radial distance of 38 µm, max amplitude 2π×2 rad/µs and max |detuning| 2π×20 rad/µs. `DigitalAnalogDevice` uses level 70, 100 atoms and 4 µm minimum spacing. `WeightedAnalogDevice` uses level 75, 256 atoms and an 80 µm radius — [_devices.py](https://github.com/pasqal-io/Pulser/blob/develop/pulser-core/pulser/devices/_devices.py). These are Pulser's example device specs, not a statement of current QPU specs.
- *1D Rydberg crystals (retired), based on Bernien et al. [arXiv:1707.04344](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.04344).* Clusters of 1–3 mutually blockaded atoms show the "Rabi frequency ... enhanced by √N". An adiabatic detuning sweep on an 11-atom chain gives Z2 order, and on a 10-atom chain with shorter spacing gives Z3 order (`QutipEmulator`) — [Building 1D Rydberg Crystals.ipynb](https://github.com/pasqal-io/Pulser/blob/develop/tutorials/retired/Building%201D%20Rydberg%20Crystals.ipynb)
- *2D antiferromagnet (retired), based on Lienhard et al. [PRX 8, 021070](https://journals.aps.org/prx/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevX.8.021070).* A square register at the blockade-radius spacing for U is swept from δ₀=−6U to δ_f=2U. The tutorial computes the connected correlation g^c(k,l) and the Néel structure factor, and points to larger-scale exponential correlation decay in [arXiv:2012.12268](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.12268.pdf) — [AF tutorial](https://github.com/pasqal-io/Pulser/blob/develop/tutorials/retired/Preparing%20state%20with%20antiferromagnetic%20order%20in%20the%20Ising%20model.ipynb)
- *Optimal control (current).* Bayesian optimisation (scikit-optimize `gp_minimize`) of interpolated Ω(t) and δ(t) to maximise S_Néel on a 12-atom ring: a 4×4 square at 5 µm spacing with the central 2×2 removed "to avoid edge effects," run with `QutipBackendV2` — [Bayesian Optimisation tutorial](https://github.com/pasqal-io/Pulser/blob/develop/tutorials/quantum_simulation/Bayesian%20Optimisation%20for%20antiferromagnetic%20state%20preparation.ipynb)
- *MWIS via QAA (current).* A 4-node weighted graph is solved with `MockDevice`, a global Rydberg channel plus a detuning-map modulator (DMM) for weights, and an adiabatic sweep with T = 40 000 ns. The atom positions are found by a minimisation heuristic that "will not work for all the graphs" — [QAA MWIS tutorial](https://github.com/pasqal-io/Pulser/blob/develop/tutorials/applications/QAA%20to%20solve%20a%20MWIS%20problem.ipynb)

**Pulser emulation methods and scale**
- Backends: local `QutipBackendV2` (QuTiP), and remote `RemoteEmuFreeBackend` ("executes the sequence on `QutipBackendV2` remotely in the cloud") and `RemoteMPSBackend`. The `MPSBackend` and `SVBackend` results carry runtime statistics — [Execution on an Emulator.ipynb](https://github.com/pasqal-io/Pulser/blob/develop/tutorials/advanced_features/Execution%20on%20an%20Emulator.ipynb)
- Pasqal emulators (PyTorch): **emu-sv** is a state vector, "numerically exact"; in noise mode it solves the full Lindblad equation. It is "typically the best choice for noiseless simulations of up to ~25 qubits," and noise mode "effectively halves the number of qubits." **emu-mps** uses MPS + TDVP, handles noise by Monte Carlo averaging, and includes DMRG. It is recommended "for larger number of qubits" — [emulators docs](https://pasqal-io.github.io/emulators/latest/)
- Paper: Bidzhiev et al., "Efficient Emulation of Neutral Atom Quantum Hardware," arXiv:2510.09813 (2025-10-10). emu-sv can emulate "up to ~27 qubits on an A100 40GB GPU," and emu-mps simulates "much larger arrays of atoms with manageable errors." Both "provide significant speed-ups over generic solvers such as QuTiP" (no number is given in the abstract) — [arXiv:2510.09813](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09813)
- The emu-mps docs page describes "larger arrays up to a hundred qubits," a "second-order two-site TDVP scheme," and noise by "Monte Carlo quantum jumps rather than solving the full Lindblad master equation." *This comes from search-result snippets only; I did not fetch the full page* — [emu-mps docs](https://pasqal-io.github.io/emulators/latest/emu_mps/)
- Emulator versions: emu-sv and emu-mps 2.9.1 (2026-08-04), with v2.10.0rc1 on 2026-09-10 — [emulators releases](https://github.com/pasqal-io/emulators/releases), [PyPI emu-mps](https://pypi.org/project/emu-mps/)

**Bloqade.jl: featured problems (eight official examples)**
- The examples are: 1 blockade, 2 adiabatic, 3 quantum scar, 4 LGT, 5 MIS, 6 MWIS, 7 QMC, 8 simulating noise — [examples](https://github.com/QuEraComputing/Bloqade.jl/tree/master/examples), [docs](https://queracomputing.github.io/Bloqade.jl/dev/)
- *Blockade.* C₆ = 2π×862690 MHz µm⁶, so the interaction is ≈2π×4 MHz at 7.74 µm. With Ω = 2π×0.5 MHz and atoms 7 µm apart, the interaction is "≈2π×7.33 MHz." The example uses a 12-site ring with Ω set from C₆/R_b⁶, the blockade subspace and `SchrodingerProblem` — [1.blockade/main.jl](https://github.com/QuEraComputing/Bloqade.jl/blob/master/examples/1.blockade/main.jl)
- *Adiabatic preparation.* Based on Bernien et al. ([Nature, doi:10.1038/nature24622](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature24622)) in 1D and Ebadi et al. ([Nature, doi:10.1038/s41586-021-03582-4](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03582-4)) in 2D: checkerboard, star and striated phases, with theory from Samajdar et al. ([PRL 124, 103601](https://journals.aps.org/prl/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevLett.124.103601)). The run uses a 9-atom chain at 5.72 µm with Ω = 2π×4 MHz and Δ from −2π×10 to +2π×10 MHz. It computes the ground-state density profile and Z2 order parameter versus Δ, then follows the ground state adiabatically — [2.adiabatic/main.jl](https://github.com/QuEraComputing/Bloqade.jl/blob/master/examples/2.adiabatic/main.jl)
- *Quantum many-body scars.* The example cites Bernien 2017, Turner et al. ([Nat. Phys., doi:10.1038/s41567-018-0137-5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41567-018-0137-5)) and the Serbyn et al. review ([Nat. Phys. 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41567-021-01230-2)). Its first part is many-body Rabi oscillations of 1–3 blockaded atoms (`KrylovEvolution`); its second part studies revival dynamics on a 9-atom chain at 5.72 µm, with the state prepared by a piecewise-linear Δ sweep (clocks 0, 0.3, 1.6, 2.2 µs; Δ/2π from −10 to +10 MHz). I did not read the exact quench protocol — [3.quantum-scar/main.jl](https://github.com/QuEraComputing/Bloqade.jl/blob/master/examples/3.quantum-scar/main.jl)
- *Lattice gauge theory.* Inspired by Surace et al. ([PRX 10, 021041](https://journals.aps.org/prx/pdf/10.1103/PhysRevX.10.021041)), the quantum link model is mapped to a 21-atom chain at 5.5 µm. Ω = 2π×5 MHz gives R_b ≈ 7.46 µm. The run uses the blockade subspace and simulates string inversion and particle–antiparticle propagation — [4.LGT/main.jl](https://github.com/QuEraComputing/Bloqade.jl/blob/master/examples/4.LGT/main.jl)
- *MIS.* Based on Pichler et al. ([arXiv:1808.10816](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10816)) and Ebadi et al. ([arXiv:2202.09372](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.09372), "up to 289 qubits"). The graph is a diagonal-connected unit-disk grid built from a 4×4 lattice at 4.5 µm with 20% random dropout and unit-disk radius 7.5 µm. The sweep runs Ω_max = 2π×4 MHz and Δ from −2π×13 to +2π×11 MHz, followed by variational optimisation — [5.MIS/main.jl](https://github.com/QuEraComputing/Bloqade.jl/blob/master/examples/5.MIS/main.jl)
- *QMC.* "While ED is numerically exact and works for smaller systems consisting of tens of atoms it scales poorly." BloqadeQMC (stochastic series expansion, adapted by Merali et al., [arXiv:2107.00766](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.00766)) "can scale to hundreds of atoms with the caveat that it is exact up to statistical errors." It is benchmarked against ED on the 9-atom Z2 chain and then run on 2D square lattices — [7.QMC/main.jl](https://github.com/QuEraComputing/Bloqade.jl/blob/master/examples/7.QMC/main.jl)
- *Noise.* Lindblad dynamics are unravelled into quantum-jump trajectories (`NoisySchrodingerProblem`, `emulate_noisy`). A single-atom Rabi example with depolarising noise and Gaussian amplitude fluctuations is checked against the analytic envelope ½ − ½e^(−4γt)cos(Ωt) — [8.simulating noise/main.jl](https://github.com/QuEraComputing/Bloqade.jl/blob/master/examples/8.simulating%20noise/main.jl)

**Bloqade emulation methods and scale**
- The Bloqade.jl modules (`lib/`) are BloqadeCUDA, BloqadeDormandPrince, BloqadeExpr, BloqadeGates, BloqadeKrylov, BloqadeLattices, BloqadeMIS, BloqadeNoisy, BloqadeODE, BloqadeQMC, BloqadeSchema, BloqadeWaveforms and YaoSubspaceArrayReg — [lib/](https://github.com/QuEraComputing/Bloqade.jl/tree/master/lib). The docs list subspace simulation "constrained by the Rydberg blockade," multithreaded CPU and CUDA GPU, MIS tools, 3-level support, QMC and noise simulation — [docs home](https://queracomputing.github.io/Bloqade.jl/dev/)
- Blockade subspace: a 3×3 lattice (9 atoms) has 512 full-space states but "63 allowed states." The docs advise that "it's safer to set the subspace radius R_s to be smaller than R_b" (e.g., R_s = R_b/2) — [subspace docs](https://queracomputing.github.io/Bloqade.jl/dev/subspace/)

The codebase also has a Julia code-generation path — [state_vector.py](https://github.com/QuEraComputing/bloqade-analog/blob/main/src/bloqade/analog/emulate/ir/state_vector.py), [space.py](https://github.com/QuEraComputing/bloqade-analog/blob/main/src/bloqade/analog/emulate/ir/space.py)

### Inferences
- Exact simulation of tens of atoms, analog-Hamiltonian physics and hundred-atom MPS emulation are all well served. Pulser plus emu-sv/emu-mps is actively released, with releases about monthly in 2026. A newcomer to this ecosystem is unlikely to win on scale.
- The *pedagogical* layer is thinning. Pulser's blockade, AF and crystal tutorials were retired in 2025 in favour of application (QAA/QUBO/MWIS) and feature tutorials. Bloqade.jl, which still has the best physics tutorial set (scars, LGT, QMC versus ED), is in maintenance mode. QuEra's maintained Python SDK has no written emulation docs. A rigorous exact/small-N study of blockade, adiabatic Z2 order and scars, with explicit convergence and error control, would cover ground the incumbents now present less well.
- The Bloqade.jl `lib/` listing has no tensor-network or MPS module. I infer that Bloqade.jl's large-N route is QMC (ground-state and thermal properties only) rather than MPS dynamics, whereas Pasqal provides MPS dynamics through emu-mps.
- The tutorial numbers are mutually consistent and give a ready-made unit check. 862690/7⁶ ≈ 7.33 and 862690/7.74⁶ ≈ 4.0 (MHz·2π), and (862690/5)^(1/6) ≈ 7.46 µm, matching the stated R_b for Ω = 2π×5 MHz. (My arithmetic.)

### Gaps
- I found no published benchmark comparing Bloqade.jl and Pulser emulator speed or accuracy. The Bloqade docs show a benchmark plot without numbers in the text.
- I did not fetch the full emu-mps page; the "up to a hundred qubits" and "2-site TDVP" claims come from search snippets.
- I could not find documented atom-count limits for Bloqade.jl full-space versus subspace emulation.
- I did not check whether the retired Pulser tutorials still render in the published docs, or whether their content (e.g., blockade) moved into the new `docs/source/*.ipynb` pages (hardware, register, noise_model).

## Q3. OpenFermion: which problems are featured, and what is its maintenance status in 2025–2026?

### Takeaway
OpenFermion, in Google's `quantumlib` GitHub organisation, is actively maintained: v1.7.1 (June 2025), v1.8.0 and v1.8.1 (July 2026), and steady commits through Sept 2026. It is an operator-algebra and compilation library, not an electronic-structure solver. Its featured problems are fermion-to-qubit mappings (JW, BK, binary codes), Hubbard and jellium models, Trotter circuits and Trotter-error bounds, low-rank factorisations, and small molecules (H₂, LiH). The H₂ dissociation curve in the tutorial is read from precomputed HDF5 files shipped with the package. Live integral generation goes through PySCF/Psi4 plugins that have not been released since December 2020.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- Over 2025–2026 OpenFermion is "maintained and modernising," not abandoned: there are releases, typing work and NumPy 2 and Cirq 1.7 compatibility. Feature growth is incremental, and the learning material is 2020-vintage.

## Q5. Which problem class is most compelling to a master's-level audience, and why?

### Takeaway
(Mostly inference.) **Rydberg-array physics** is the strongest single showcase: blockade, then adiabatic Z2 order, then scars. A two-level atom with a 1/r⁶ interaction is textbook quantum mechanics, the key quantities are derivable, and exact numerics on 10–25 atoms decide the answers. **Transmon spectra plus DRAG leakage dynamics** is a close second and the clearest gap, because the incumbents cover the statics but not the dynamics. **H₂ dissociation** is the most recognisable but numerically the least decisive.

### Cited Findings
- Rydberg concepts reduce to master's-level QM. The two-atom H = (Ω₁/2)σˣ₁ + (Ω₂/2)σˣ₂ + U n₁n₂, and the blockade suppresses |rr⟩ below a radius — [Pulser blockade tutorial](https://github.com/pasqal-io/Pulser/blob/develop/tutorials/retired/intro_rydberg_blockade.ipynb). The radius is R_b = (C₆/Ω)^{1/6} in code — [Pulser _device_datacls.py](https://github.com/pasqal-io/Pulser/blob/develop/pulser-core/pulser/devices/_device_datacls.py). The √N enhancement of collective Rabi frequency is a clean numerical signature — [Pulser crystals tutorial](https://github.com/pasqal-io/Pulser/blob/develop/tutorials/retired/Building%201D%20Rydberg%20Crystals.ipynb)
- The Hilbert-space constraint is explicit and countable: 512 states become 63 for a 3×3 lattice — [Bloqade subspace docs](https://queracomputing.github.io/Bloqade.jl/dev/subspace/). Exact diagonalisation "works for smaller systems consisting of tens of atoms" and "scales poorly" — [Bloqade QMC tutorial](https://github.com/QuEraComputing/Bloqade.jl/blob/master/examples/7.QMC/main.jl). Exact GPU state vectors reach about 25–27 qubits — [Pasqal emulators docs](https://pasqal-io.github.io/emulators/latest/), [arXiv:2510.09813](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09813)
- Scars link to a celebrated experiment and to a nontrivial theory concept (non-ergodic special eigenstates) — [Bloqade scar tutorial](https://github.com/QuEraComputing/Bloqade.jl/blob/master/examples/3.quantum-scar/main.jl)
- The transmon is a particle in a cosine potential with an offset-charge parameter. The trade-off is exponential charge-dispersion suppression against only a power-law loss of anharmonicity — [Koch et al. 2007](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0703002). Hamiltonians in charge and phase form are given in [demo_transmon](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits-examples/blob/master/examples/demo_transmon.ipynb) and [demo_fluxonium](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits-examples/blob/master/examples/demo_fluxonium.ipynb)
- DRAG has a simple analytic recipe: a derivative quadrature control that cancels leakage — [Motzoi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/0901.0534). The maintained incumbents do not feature it (Q1 and Q4), and Qiskit Dynamics is deprecated — [qiskit-dynamics](https://github.com/qiskit-community/qiskit-dynamics)
- H₂ is a 4-qubit JW problem, demonstrated through shipped data — [OpenFermion intro](https://github.com/quantumlib/OpenFermion/blob/master/docs/tutorials/intro_to_openfermion.ipynb), [workshop](https://github.com/quantumlib/OpenFermion/blob/master/docs/tutorials/intro_workshop_exercises.ipynb)

### Inferences
- **Ranking for "numerics materially determines the answer" combined with master's-level accessibility:**
  1. *Rydberg blockade to adiabatic order to scars.* Students can derive R_b and the √N law analytically and then need numerics for gap closing during the sweep, adiabaticity versus sweep time, finite-size Z2 order parameters and scar revivals versus thermalisation. It is tied to famous experiments: Bernien et al. 2017 and Ebadi et al. 2021, both linked from the Bloqade tutorials. However, incumbents are strong at scale and actively maintained (Pulser/emu-mps), so the competitive angle is exactness, transparency and pedagogy, not atom count.
  2. *Transmon and fluxonium: from static spectra to driven dynamics.* The static part (spectra versus n_g and flux, dispersion versus E_J/E_C) is well served by scqubits and so offers parity at best. Pulse-level leakage, DRAG, multi-level rotating-frame dynamics and Lindblad T1/T2 effects during gates are now unserved by a maintained incumbent tutorial. Numerics decide leakage versus gate time quantitatively. This is the highest-value gap.
  3. *Fermionic mappings, Trotter error and H₂/LiH.* The algebra of JW versus BK and of Trotter-error bounds is pedagogically rich. The H₂ curve itself is a 16×16 diagonalisation (FCI in a minimal basis) whose interesting physics, the failure of HF at stretched bond lengths, is already printed in OpenFermion's shipped data. It works best as a correctness benchmark or an entry point to Trotter-error scaling, not as a numerics showcase.
- Each class needs only a short list of hardware and control concepts at master's level. For Rydberg arrays: Ω, Δ, C₆/r⁶ and the blockade radius. For superconducting qubits: E_J, E_C, n_g, Φ_ext, anharmonicity and the rotating frame/DRAG quadrature. For chemistry: second quantisation, JW strings and STO-3G active spaces. All appear explicitly in the cited tutorials, so the explanations can be anchored to incumbent material.

### Gaps
- I found no survey or teaching study measuring which of these topics master's students find most compelling; the ranking above is reasoned inference.
- I did not find incumbent tutorials that combine control and noise for transmons (pulse plus Lindblad) to set a quantitative accuracy bar for such a study.
