## 1. Which failure modes are specific to quantum numerics, and how is each detected?

### Takeaway
Six failure families recur. (i) Time stepping loses structure (norm or trace, positivity, Hermiticity), and step control misses drives or click times. (ii) Truncating infinite-dimensional spaces can converge slowly, induce stiffness, or converge to the wrong dynamics. (iii) Matrix functions and degenerate or non-normal eigenproblems fail. (iv) Trajectory methods carry statistical and event-location error. (v) Tensor networks carry SVD, projection and Trotter error, and no library checks convergence automatically. (vi) Floating point: single-precision GPU defaults and 1−F cancellation put a floor of roughly 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁷ on the infidelity that can be resolved. A central obstacle for any external checker is that libraries often repair invariants cosmetically. QuTiP renormalizes output "to hide ODE numerical errors", and Dynamiqs evolves only the Hermitian part, so checking invariants on returned states can be vacuous unless the checker controls those options or reads raw integrator output.

### Cited Findings

#### Time integration of the Schrödinger and Lindblad equations
- Riesch & Jirauschek establish "the criterion that only methods that have a completely positive trace preserving (CPTP) update map can be used in long-term simulations". They assess the three most widely used methods (matrix exponential, Runge–Kutta, predictor–corrector) and "demonstrate that only the update step of the matrix exponential method is a CPTP map" — [arXiv:1808.00416](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.00416); [J. Comput. Phys. version](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0021999119302396). A search summary of the same work adds that classical RK methods fail to preserve positivity "even for Hamiltonian evolution" — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.00416) (search summary).
- A literature of positivity-preserving Lindblad integrators has followed: Cao & Lu's structure-preserving schemes ([arXiv:2103.01194](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01194); [J. Sci. Comput.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10915-024-02707-x)); Appelö & Cheng's high-order, low-rank "Kraus is King" method, which "preserves complete positivity and [is] trace preserving" ([arXiv:2409.08898](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08898)); CPTP schemes with tensor-train compression ([arXiv:2605.01494](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.01494), search summary); and arbitrary-order low-rank CPTP schemes for time-dependent Hamiltonians built from nested Picard iterations ([arXiv:2511.12012](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.12012), search summary).
- Robin, Rouchon & Sellem: with an unbounded Lindbladian, "projecting the evolution onto a finite-dimensional subspace using a Galerkin approximation inherently introduces stiffness, leading to a Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy type condition for explicit integration schemes". They prove convergence of explicit schemes that "correspond to quantum channels and thus preserve … linearity, complete positivity and trace" — [arXiv:2503.01712](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01712).
- Norm and trace drift are hidden by default. QuTiP's `sesolve` and `mesolve` set `normalize_output: True`, documented as "Normalize output state to hide ODE numerical errors" — [sesolve.py L187–188](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/blob/56ed7854177e046124118fdcb6b958d557b65984/qutip/solver/sesolve.py#L187-L188); [mesolve.py L110–111, L211](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/blob/56ed7854177e046124118fdcb6b958d557b65984/qutip/solver/mesolve.py#L110-L111). QuTiP's own test of this option evolves under a non-Hermitian H and asserts that the returned traces equal 1 to `atol=1e-15`. It therefore tests the renormalization, not the integrator — [test_mesolve.py L268–291](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/blob/56ed7854177e046124118fdcb6b958d557b65984/qutip/tests/solver/test_mesolve.py#L268-L291).
- The cosmetic repair can itself corrupt results. In QuTiP 5.0.1 a user's off-diagonal coherence failed to decay, and a maintainer explained: "Qutip 5 normalize the density matrices per default. When the initial density matrix trace is not 1, that normalization breaks the evolution" (fix: `normalize_output=False`) — [QuTiP #2426](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/2426).
- Hermiticity is imposed by construction. Dynamiqs' `mesolve` defaults to `assume_hermitian=True`, which "allows to halve the number of matrix multiplications … since only the Hermitian part of `rho` is evolved" — [mesolve.py L188–191](https://github.com/dynamiqs/dynamiqs/blob/58a6baa385f6824fe8ac107b9b26935f19ee6bc6/dynamiqs/integrators/apis/mesolve.py#L188-L191).
- Cached invariant flags can lie. QuTiP states returned by `mesolve` reported `isherm == True` when they were not Hermitian, both under a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian and under a Liouvillian that does not preserve Hermiticity — [QuTiP #2410](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/2410). Regression tests now assert that the flag "must follow the evolved data, not the initial state" — [test_mesolve.py L979–1015](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/blob/56ed7854177e046124118fdcb6b958d557b65984/qutip/tests/solver/test_mesolve.py#L979-L1015).
- Normalization is sometimes deliberately left to users. QuantumOptics.jl's semiclassical MCWF passes unnormalized states to the classical equations. A maintainer declined to normalize: "It's not possible to know when (or if) the user needs a normalized state. Normalizing regardless of this hurts performance. So documenting it will have to do." — [QuantumOptics.jl #269](https://github.com/qojulia/QuantumOptics.jl/issues/269).
- Drives and step control, beyond the pulse-skipping reports in the earlier report ([QuTiP #1265](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/1265), [#2814](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/2814)): for a π pulse, a ~1000 ns wait and a second π pulse, one commenter wrote that "mesolve, which uses ZVODE … raises no warning" and returns a wrong result, while LSODA was correct — [QuTiP #1038](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/1038). Users cannot always tell an artefact from physics: a reporter took persistent oscillations in a dissipative, doubly driven two-level system to be unphysical, and the maintainer answered that such beatings come from counter-rotating terms and are normal — [QuTiP #2356](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/2356).
- Tolerance and precision interact. Dynamiqs warns that in single precision, setting `rtol` or `atol` below the defaults can slow simulations, which "may even get stuck", and recommends switching to double precision — [Dynamiqs sharp bits](https://www.dynamiqs.org/stable/documentation/getting_started/sharp-bits.html).
- Event location and dense output. Dynamiqs' event-based jump method warns that adaptive solvers "may choose to take very large step sizes, which results in imprecise click times" unless `dtmax` or a `root_finder` is set. By default the click-time precision is "determined by the integration step size" — [method.py L572–593](https://github.com/dynamiqs/dynamiqs/blob/58a6baa385f6824fe8ac107b9b26935f19ee6bc6/dynamiqs/method.py#L572-L593). QuTiP's `mcsolve` locates collapse times with defaults `norm_tol=1e-4` ("Tolerance in norm"), `norm_t_tol=1e-6` ("Tolerance in time") and at most `norm_steps=25` tries — [mcsolve.py L879–886](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/blob/56ed7854177e046124118fdcb6b958d557b65984/qutip/solver/mcsolve.py#L879-L886).
- A posteriori error control exists in the time-dependent Schrödinger literature. Kormann, Holmgren & Karlsson built h,p-adaptive Magnus–Lanczos propagators that exploit self-adjointness for "a low-cost global error estimate" and control the global error "by only solving the equation once" (J. Chem. Phys. 2008) — [Uppsala thesis](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:549981/FULLTEXT01.pdf) (search summary). Auzinger, Koch & Thalhammer developed defect-based local error estimators for splitting methods applied to Schrödinger equations (Parts I–III, 2012–2014) — [Numer. Algorithms](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11075-014-9935-8) (search summary). A posteriori estimators also exist for Magnus-type integrators — [M2AN](http://archive.numdam.org/articles/10.1051/m2an/2018050/) (title only).
- Order reduction occurs with unbounded operators. Burgarth et al. show that operator-norm Trotter bounds "become trivial for unbounded operators". For states with fat-tailed energy distributions, such as low-angular-momentum hydrogen states, "the Trotter error scales worse than expected (sublinearly) in the number of Trotter steps", and "the higher-order Trotter hierarchy breaks down" for some states, including the hydrogen ground state — [arXiv:2312.08044](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.08044) (Phys. Rev. Research 6, 043155).

#### Hilbert-space and Fock truncation
- Fischer, Burgarth & Lonigro show that, under mild assumptions, truncated Hamiltonians converge to the Friedrichs extension of H restricted to finite combinations of basis vectors. That extension "crucially depends on the choice of basis" and can differ from H, in which case "there is no numerical test that can reveal this failure, unless one has the analytical solution to compare with". For a particle in a box and a wide class of bases (associated Legendre polynomials, for instance), the truncated dynamics "will always converge to the one corresponding to the particle with Dirichlet boundary conditions, regardless the initial choice of boundary conditions" — [arXiv:2412.15889](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15889); [Quantum (Jan 2026)](https://quantum-journal.org/papers/q-2026-01-27-1985/).
- Tong, Albert, McClean, Preskill & Su bound the error of truncating bosons and gauge fields. If the initial state has low local quantum numbers, a truncation error of at most ε needs only a cutoff Λ growing polylogarithmically in 1/ε, "an exponential improvement over previous bounds based on energy conservation". Spectrally isolated eigenstates are covered too, and the authors compute numerical upper bounds on Λ for the Hubbard–Holstein model — [arXiv:2110.06942](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.06942); [Quantum 6, 816 (2022)](https://quantum-journal.org/papers/q-2022-09-22-816/) (abstract via search summary).
- Woods, Cramer & Plenio derive "rigorous truncation-error bounds for the spin-boson model and its generalizations" and "super-exponential Lieb–Robinson-type bounds on the error when restricting the bath to finitely-many modes". They show that "the error introduced by truncating the local Hilbert spaces may be efficiently monitored numerically", which allows "fully certified" TEDOPA simulations — [arXiv:1504.01531](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.01531).
- Etienney, Robin & Rouchon give bounds on both the Hilbert-space truncation and the time discretization of the Lindblad equation that "can be explicitly computed to guarantee the accuracy of the numerical results", with "the tightness of the upper bound" highlighted empirically. The bounds let the truncation be adjusted dynamically ("fully adaptive simulations"), which "relieves users of the challenge of selecting an appropriate truncation". Unitary dynamics is covered as a special case — [arXiv:2501.09607](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.09607); [Quantum 10, 2031 (2026)](https://quantum-journal.org/papers/q-2026-03-16-2031/).
- What a shipping adaptive truncation actually checks. QuantumToolbox.jl's dynamical Fock dimension grows a mode when a tail-population heuristic, the 1-norm of the last `pillow` diagonal entries of the reduced density matrix times √dim / pillow, exceeds `tol`, and shrinks it below `tol·1e-2`. This is a heuristic, not a bound — [time_evolution_dynamical.jl L61–88](https://github.com/qutip/QuantumToolbox.jl/blob/fd3dedb7de5331037afaf7a0e69c9e8225d526c9/src/time_evolution/time_evolution_dynamical.jl#L61-L88). Its tests accept a summed relative deviation from a fixed-cutoff reference below 0.01 — [dynamical_fock_dimension_mesolve.jl L47](https://github.com/qutip/QuantumToolbox.jl/blob/fd3dedb7de5331037afaf7a0e69c9e8225d526c9/test/main-test/dynamical_fock_dimension_mesolve.jl#L47). The dynamical-shifted-Fock tests accept normalized squared deviations below 0.1 and 0.6 — [dynamical-shifted-fock.jl L71–72, L151–154](https://github.com/qutip/QuantumToolbox.jl/blob/fd3dedb7de5331037afaf7a0e69c9e8225d526c9/test/main-test/dynamical-shifted-fock.jl#L71-L72).
- A grep of the QuTiP, QuantumOptics.jl, QuantumToolbox.jl and Dynamiqs source trees for "a posteriori", "error bound" or "truncation error" found no implementation of computable truncation or time-discretization bounds — local grep at the pinned commits ([QuantumToolbox.jl src](https://github.com/qutip/QuantumToolbox.jl/tree/fd3dedb7de5331037afaf7a0e69c9e8225d526c9/src); [Dynamiqs src](https://github.com/dynamiqs/dynamiqs/tree/58a6baa385f6824fe8ac107b9b26935f19ee6bc6/dynamiqs)).
- Already documented in the earlier report: scqubits' default charge cutoff cost one user eight months ([scqubits #274](https://github.com/scqubits/scqubits/issues/274)); Strawberry Fields truncates after computing exact elements while QuTiP and QuantumOptics.jl exponentiate the truncated generator ([QuTiP states.py](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/blob/master/qutip/core/states.py)); Piquasso added a "cutoff not high enough" check only in 2025 ([Piquasso #472](https://github.com/Budapest-Quantum-Computing-Group/piquasso/issues/472)).

#### Matrix functions and eigenproblems
- Moler & Van Loan: the matrix exponential can be computed in many ways, but "in practice, consideration of computational stability and efficiency indicates that some of the methods are preferable to others but none are completely satisfactory" — [SIAM Review 2003](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1137/S00361445024180) (abstract via search summary).
- Krylov exponentials come with error control of varying rigor. Expokit performs a posteriori error control "following the procedures of ODEs solvers" — [Expokit paper](https://www.maths.uq.edu.au/expokit/paper.pdf) (search summary). TimeEvolver's a posteriori bound is, "apart from effects of numerical roundoff, … rigorous, which represents a crucial novelty as compared to existing software packages such as Expokit"; it relies on Hermiticity and targets sparse problems beyond dimension 10⁶ — [arXiv:2205.15346](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15346).
- Verified enclosures exist for small matrices. Miyajima gives two algorithms for interval matrices that contain exp(A): one based on spectral decomposition at cubic cost, one based on Jordan decomposition for defective matrices — [Adv. Comput. Math. 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10444-018-9609-5) (search summary); see also [Computing Enclosures for the Matrix Exponential](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1137/19M1263431). A search summary states that computing a sharp enclosure of the interval-matrix exponential is NP-hard, apparently from [Goldsztejn & Neumaier, arXiv:0908.3954](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.3954) (attribution not verified).
- QuTiP's sparse eigensolver mishandles purely imaginary spectra: `(num(4)*1j).eigenenergies(sparse=True)` returned 1j and 3j twice each and missed 0 and 2j. The issue is still open, and a maintainer wrote: "The issue with the sparse solver is not solved, but it's used less" — [QuTiP #1998](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/1998).
- ITensor's `eigen` for block-sparse tensors "incorrectly assumes sorted eigenvalues" (open; title read only) — [ITensors.jl #1655](https://github.com/ITensor/ITensors.jl/issues/1655).
- Non-normal Liouvillians mislead spectral reasoning. Mori & Shirai show that in boundary-dissipated many-body systems the relaxation time "is determined not by the gap or low-lying eigenvalues of the Liouvillian but by superexponentially large expansion coefficients for Liouvillian eigenvectors with non-small eigenvalues" — [arXiv:2006.10953](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10953) (PRL 2020; search summary).
- Already documented in the earlier report: QuTiP's eigen steady-state method "silently returns a completely wrong, non-physical state" for a composite system ([QuTiP #3026](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/3026)); QuantumOptics.jl's truncated-exponential operator was off by 1.37×10¹² at dimension 200 ([QuantumOptics.jl #281](https://github.com/qojulia/QuantumOptics.jl/issues/281)); a PIQS Liouvillian spectrum bug has been open since 2019 ([QuTiP #993](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/993)).

#### Quantum trajectories and Monte Carlo wavefunctions
- Dynamiqs' fixed-step stochastic integrator computed its number of 1000-step chunks with `round()`, so when `nsteps % 1000 >= 500` it integrated nsteps + 1000 steps. A maintainer replied: "Oh, that's pretty bad. We really need tests for stochastic solvers..." — [Dynamiqs #1092](https://github.com/dynamiqs/dynamiqs/issues/1092).
- Statistical oracles in the test suites are loose. QuTiP compares `mcsolve` with 500 trajectories against expected values using `assert_allclose(..., rtol=tol)` — [test_mcsolve.py L28–55](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/blob/56ed7854177e046124118fdcb6b958d557b65984/qutip/tests/solver/test_mcsolve.py#L28-L55). QuantumOptics.jl averages 500 MCWF trajectories and requires trace distance below 0.1 from the master-equation solution at each time — [test_timeevolution_mcwf.jl L164–177](https://github.com/qojulia/QuantumOptics.jl/blob/f9fd606a8cb9bf0cfb821786117253cbdc110911/test/test_timeevolution_mcwf.jl#L164-L177). QuantumToolbox.jl checks `mcsolve` against an analytic decay with `atol = 1.0e-2 * length(tlist_q)` — [time_dependent.jl L85–89](https://github.com/qutip/QuantumToolbox.jl/blob/fd3dedb7de5331037afaf7a0e69c9e8225d526c9/test/main-test/basic_solvers/time_dependent.jl#L85-L89).
- QuTiP measures the empirical convergence order of its stochastic integrators: it fits log error against log step size and asserts `(order + 0.25) < error_order` — [test_sode_method.py L99–173](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/blob/56ed7854177e046124118fdcb6b958d557b65984/qutip/tests/solver/test_sode_method.py#L99-L173).

#### Tensor networks
- ITensor defines `cutoff` as "the desired truncation error of the SVD, by default defined as the sum of the squares of the smallest singular values" — [matrix_decomposition.jl L82–83](https://github.com/ITensor/ITensors.jl/blob/0b76d283550b25f9928a3f614cd0680ac90e0b95/src/tensor_operations/matrix_decomposition.jl#L82-L83). Its DMRG observer records the maximum truncation error per sweep — [observer.jl L134–175](https://github.com/ITensor/ITensorMPS.jl/blob/2a98a9902948f8e07bc52dcf07a8a81a9f3caac5/src/observer.jl#L134-L175).
- ITensorMPS's FAQ states that "there is no automatic or bulletproof check for DMRG convergence". Its heuristics are to run more sweeps, see whether the bond dimension saturates `maxdim`, inspect the reported maximum truncation error, and compute the energy variance — [DMRG FAQ](https://github.com/ITensor/ITensorMPS.jl/blob/2a98a9902948f8e07bc52dcf07a8a81a9f3caac5/docs/src/faq/DMRG.md?plain=1#L3-L46).
- Hubig, Haegeman & Schollwöck introduce a cheap approximation to the variance ⟨(H−E)²⟩ that supports extrapolating observables to zero error, "on-par with extrapolation using 2DMRG truncation error or the full variance at a fraction of the computational effort" — [arXiv:1711.01104](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.01104) (search summary).
- Paeckel et al.'s review lists TDVP's error sources: projection error from the finite bond dimension, an O(δ³)-per-step time-step error, truncation error in two-site TDVP, and Krylov solver error. Truncation error "can be controlled and measured by the discarded weight", "affects both unitarity of evolution and conserved quantities", and is checked by increasing the bond dimension; projection error is "particularly problematic" for global quenches from product states under non-local Hamiltonians — [arXiv:1901.05824](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05824) (search summary).
- quimb's TEBD `err` is a heuristic Trotter estimate. Each step adds `self._ham_norm * dt ** (order + 1)` with `_ham_norm = H.mean_norm()`, and `choose_time_step` inverts "Trotter error is ~ (T / dt) * dt^(order + 1)". SVD truncation error is not included — [tebd.py L284–287](https://github.com/jcmgray/quimb/blob/8120ab0a3e3b5a98bfb26de72d63d17065a3230a/quimb/tensor/tn1d/tebd.py#L284-L287), [L307–315](https://github.com/jcmgray/quimb/blob/8120ab0a3e3b5a98bfb26de72d63d17065a3230a/quimb/tensor/tn1d/tebd.py#L307-L315), [L445–447](https://github.com/jcmgray/quimb/blob/8120ab0a3e3b5a98bfb26de72d63d17065a3230a/quimb/tensor/tn1d/tebd.py#L445-L447).
- Contraction order can change answers in approximate contraction. In quimb, belief-propagation contraction gave different results depending on the order of partial contractions, and a Bell-state ⟨ZZ⟩ came out wrong. The maintainer explained that in loopy settings "BP can be sensitive to the initial messages and the update order, and also fail to converge", and warnings were added — [quimb #325](https://github.com/jcmgray/quimb/issues/325).
- GPU linear algebra can fail silently. On GPU, cuSOLVER's SVD "silently produces NaN for rectangular rank-deficient matrices" during TDVP with mixed site dimensions (a qubit coupled to oscillators of dimension 13); the failure surfaces later as an error in the KrylovKit exponential (open, May 2026) — [ITensors.jl #1753](https://github.com/ITensor/ITensors.jl/issues/1753). An open ITensor issue is titled "Contracting with DiagBlockSparse can give the wrong answer" — [ITensors.jl #1477](https://github.com/ITensor/ITensors.jl/issues/1477) (title only).
- Low-precision contraction is limited by exponent range, not mantissa. Ootomo et al. write that "the exponent range is a critical issue" for tensor-core GEMM in circuit simulation and that "FP16 supports a much smaller exponent range". They choose tensor cores from the exponent statistics of the input tensors and report up to 1.86× throughput on random-circuit-sampling benchmarks, Sycamore circuits included — [arXiv:2303.08989](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08989). quimb exposes `strip_exponent`, which returns "the overall exponent of the contraction, in log10, as well as the 'mantissa'", together with `equalize_norms` — [tensor_core.py L1945–1949](https://github.com/jcmgray/quimb/blob/8120ab0a3e3b5a98bfb26de72d63d17065a3230a/quimb/tensor/tensor_core.py#L1945-L1949).
- The theoretical licence for MPS time evolution is locality. Osborne showed that the propagator of a 1D spin chain can be approximated to error ε with resources polynomial in n and ε but exponential in |t| — [quant-ph/0508031](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0508031).

#### Floating point: single vs double, cancellation, small infidelities
- Single precision is the default in GPU-oriented tools. Dynamiqs represents all objects "by default with single-precision floating-point numbers (`float32` or `complex64`), whereas the default in QuTiP or NumPy is double-precision" — [Dynamiqs sharp bits](https://www.dynamiqs.org/stable/documentation/getting_started/sharp-bits.html). CUDA-Q documents that "The `nvidia` target has single-precision as the default setting", with fp64 available as a target option — [CUDA-Q state-vector simulators](https://nvidia.github.io/cuda-quantum/latest/using/backends/sims/svsims.html).
- Invariant checks misfire when their tolerances ignore precision. Cirq's `simulate_expectation_values` raised a ValueError on a 4-qubit circuit because `expectation_from_state_vector` checks its preconditions by default — [Cirq #5268](https://github.com/quantumlib/Cirq/issues/5268).
- Roundoff accumulates under repeated channels. In Cirq's density-matrix simulator the trace "decreases and eventually approaches 0" over repeated gates and measurements. A maintainer traced this to an `einsum` in `partial_trace` that is "losing precision and ending up with a not-quite-one trace"; the issue has been open since 2022 — [Cirq #5916](https://github.com/quantumlib/Cirq/issues/5916).
- Already documented in the earlier report: QuTiP returns `fidelity(rho, rho)` = 1.0000000102 for a rank-deficient state ([QuTiP #3024](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/3024)); Cirq's state vector is not normalized ([Cirq #6402](https://github.com/quantumlib/Cirq/issues/6402)); PennyLane's probabilities do not sum to 1 ([PennyLane #9000](https://github.com/PennyLaneAI/pennylane/issues/9000)); and Float64 reports a double-well splitting of ~10⁻¹³ against a true ~10⁻²³ ([QuantumToolbox.jl precision guide](https://qutip.org/QuantumToolbox.jl/stable/users_guide/arbitrary_precision)).

### Inferences
- *Detection map (inference from the findings above):*
  - **Norm or trace drift**: recompute norm or trace from raw states with renormalization off. A naive post-hoc check on QuTiP output sees exactly 1 by design. An external tool can do this cheaply if it controls solver options.
  - **Positivity**: compute the minimum eigenvalue of ρ(t). This is feasible densely for d up to roughly 10⁴, but needs extra machinery for low-rank or MPS states. None of the six suites checks it (see Q2).
  - **Hermiticity**: recompute ‖ρ−ρ†‖ rather than trusting flags (QuTiP #2410) or by-construction guarantees (Dynamiqs), which make the check vacuous.
  - **Pulse skipping and discontinuities**: sweep tolerances and `max_step`; instrument right-hand-side evaluation times to confirm that every drive window was sampled; compare against integration split at breakpoints. This is cheap at teaching scale and needs no physics knowledge.
  - **Stiffness**: watch integrator statistics (rejected steps, step-size collapse) and cross-check explicit against implicit methods. Robin et al. show that truncation itself creates stiffness, so stiffness diagnostics double as truncation diagnostics.
  - **Truncation**: run cutoff sweeps with tail-population monitors, ideally backed by a computable bound (Etienney et al.). Fischer et al. prove that sweeps can converge to the wrong limit, which no numerical check reveals; that case needs analysis or an analytic reference.
  - **Matrix exponentials and eigenproblems**: compare two algorithms (Padé, Krylov, eigendecomposition), compute residuals ‖Hv−λv‖ and ‖Lρ‖, check kernel dimension (to detect multiple steady states), and compare degenerate eigenspaces as projectors rather than vectors. Non-normal Liouvillians need eigenvector condition numbers or pseudospectra, not just eigenvalues.
  - **Trajectories**: check confidence intervals for 1/√N scaling against the master equation at small size, seed reproducibility, and consistency checks such as "steps taken = steps requested", which would have caught Dynamiqs #1092.
  - **Tensor networks**: sweep bond dimension and time step, account for discarded weight (quimb's `err` omits it), compute energy variance, compare with exact results at small L, and compare BP against exact or alternative contractions.
  - **Floating point**: sweep precision, and compute infidelity in a cancellation-free form.
- *Local check (not a cited source; numpy 2.4.2, Python 3.14, run this session):* for two 1024-dimensional states with exact infidelity set to 10⁻³, 10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁷, 10⁻⁹ and 10⁻¹¹:
  - Computing 1−|⟨a|b⟩|² in complex64 returned 9.66×10⁻⁶ for a true 10⁻⁵, and exactly 0 for 10⁻⁷, 10⁻⁹ and 10⁻¹¹.
  - Complex128 reproduced all five values to about three digits.
  - Computing the same quantity as the squared norm of the residual b − ⟨a|b⟩a recovered all five values even in complex64.
  - Over 20,000 random two-qubit gates on 12 qubits, the complex64 state's norm error stayed of order 10⁻⁷ (float32 resolution). Its infidelity against the complex128 run was of order 10⁻⁷ in magnitude; one sample was negative because the complex64 state is not exactly normalized. The complex128 norm error grew to 4.4×10⁻¹³.
  - Implication: in single precision, the naive formula cannot resolve infidelities below about 10⁻⁶ no matter how accurate the solver is. In double precision the arithmetic floor sits far below 10⁻⁹, so the binding limit becomes the integrator tolerances (relative 10⁻⁶, absolute 10⁻⁸ in QuTiP, QuantumToolbox.jl and QuantumOptics.jl, per the earlier report). How the metric is formulated matters as much as the working precision.
- *Inference:* library designs that repair invariants (renormalizing output, evolving only the Hermitian part) are sensible for end users but disable the cheapest verification signal. An external tool must switch them off, or measure how large the repair was, before invariant checks mean anything.

### Gaps
- No primary source was found that quantifies roundoff growth as a function of tensor-network contraction order. The material found concerns exponent range (Ootomo et al.), mixed precision, and the sensitivity of approximate contraction to BP message order (quimb #325), not roundoff.
- The accuracy of DifferentialEquations.jl's dense-output and `saveat` interpolation, which QuantumOptics.jl and QuantumToolbox.jl inherit, was not verified: the documentation fetch returned nothing and the search budget ran out.
- Driven-transmon ionization and rotating-wave breakdown, which are model-truncation failures rather than numerical ones, were not researched this session.
- No study was found measuring how often positivity loss in RK or Bloch–Redfield integration changes a published physical conclusion.

## 2. How do practitioners and libraries verify quantum simulation results today?

### Takeaway
Verification inside the main libraries is test-suite based, and its oracles are weak. The tests compare against a handful of analytic models (exponential decay, a damped qubit, vacuum Rabi oscillation, harmonic-oscillator and driven-cavity steady states, two-level propagators) at tolerances ranging from 10⁻¹⁵ to 0.6. They add cross-method checks: master equation against exp(Lt), trajectory ensembles against the master equation, TEBD and TDVP against dense exponentials. None of the six suites examined checks positivity of solver output or uses property-based testing. Only QuTiP measures convergence order, and only for stochastic integrators. Solution verification (convergence in cutoff, time step, bond dimension) is left to users; ITensor states plainly that there is "no automatic or bulletproof check". Outside the libraries, a rigorous toolkit exists but is largely absent from production code. It includes computable Lindblad truncation and time-step bounds (2026), rigorous Krylov bounds, boson-truncation bounds, commutator-scaling Trotter bounds, Lieb–Robinson finite-size bounds, interval enclosures for small matrices, and formal verification of compilers and simple ODE integrators. The strongest cross-solver culture is in many-body physics, where multi-method benchmarks set community reference values.

#### Cross-solver and cross-method comparison
- CERTIFY-ED (a two-author 2026 preprint) describes current exact-diagonalization practice: results "are rarely accompanied by machine-checkable evidence of their numerical correctness", and the community "relies on the implicit trust chain LAPACK → user code → result, with at most informal agreement against another package treated as confirmation". Its proposed layer, which runs alongside QuSpin, XDiag or ALPS, has five parts:
  - a multi-oracle eigensolver over "three independent LAPACK paths";
  - "thirteen logically independent validation layers covering algebraic invariants, analytic limits, alternative algorithms, arbitrary-precision reference computation, conservation laws, dynamical consistency, and finite-size scaling";
  - SHA-256-hashed certificates;
  - an error-injection layer for six error classes;
  - reported results: maximum disagreement with QuSpin of 1.6×10⁻¹⁴ over 320 eigenvalues, and agreement with 50-digit `mpmath` to 1.6×10⁻¹⁵.

  — [arXiv:2605.11787](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11787)
- Many-body multi-method benchmarks. LeBlanc et al. ran auxiliary-field and diagrammatic QMC, DMET, DMRG, DCA, fixed-node DMC, coupled cluster and other methods on the 2D Hubbard model; "comparison of results obtained by different methods allows for the identification of uncertainties and systematic errors", with emphasis on extrapolating to the thermodynamic limit — [arXiv:1505.02290](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.02290). Motta et al.'s hydrogen-chain study used "exhaustive cross-checks and validation" and gave "a confidence bound … on all uncertainties" — [arXiv:1705.01608](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.01608).
- Cross-method disagreement at the frontier. For IBM's 127-qubit kicked Ising circuits, Anand et al. "observe a discrepancy of up to 20% among the different classical approaches so far, an uncertainty comparable to the bootstrapped error bars of the experiment" — [arXiv:2306.17839](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17839). Begušić, Gray & Chan later converged these observables to "an absolute accuracy, without extrapolation, … of <0.01", using an effective bond dimension above 16,000,000 — [arXiv:2308.05077](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05077).
- In open-system software, the earlier report found that cross-library benchmarks time fixed configurations and never score error against a converged reference ([QuantumToolbox.jl paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.21440)).

#### Convergence studies, extrapolation and a posteriori estimates
- DMRG: ITensor's heuristics and Hubig et al.'s variance extrapolation (cited in Q1).
- Trotter extrapolation: Rendon, Watkins & Wiebe show that polynomial interpolation of Trotterized expectation values gives circuit depth O(T^(1+1/p) polylog(1/ε)), "an exponential improvement in precision over product formulas alone" — [Quantum 8, 1266 (2024)](https://quantum-journal.org/papers/q-2024-02-26-1266/); [Zapata summary](https://zapata.ai/publications/improved-error-scaling-for-trotter-simulations-through-extrapolation/) (search summary).
- Empirical over rigorous: Childs, Maslov, Nam, Ross & Su found that "higher-order product formulas prevail if empirical error estimates suffice" — [arXiv:1711.10980](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.10980). A search summary of the PNAS paper adds that the commutator bound "can be very loose", so the authors extrapolate errors from direct classical simulation of small instances; this carries no rigorous guarantee but cuts product-formula costs by several orders of magnitude — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1801723115) (search summary).
- A posteriori estimates (cited in Q1): Etienney et al. for Lindblad truncation and time stepping, Kormann et al. for global TDSE error, Auzinger et al. for splitting methods, Expokit, TimeEvolver, and Woods et al.'s truncation monitoring.

#### Rigorous error bounds
- Commutator Trotter bounds: Childs, Su, Tran, Wiebe & Zhu's bound for the 1D Heisenberg model with even–odd ordering "overestimates the complexity by only a factor of 5" — [PRX 11, 011020](https://link.aps.org/pdf/10.1103/PhysRevX.11.011020) (abstract via search summary).
- Tighter evaluation: Blunt, Ivanov & Bay-Smidt note that evaluating nested-commutator bounds "by repeated application of the triangle inequality, significantly loosen[s] the bound". They bound spectral norms with sign-problem-free operators computed by projector Monte Carlo; the result is "often extremely tight, and even exact in some instances", and cuts the Trotter cost for the uniform electron gas by an order of magnitude — [arXiv:2510.11621](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11621).
- Unbounded operators: Burgarth et al. (cited in Q1). Truncation bounds: Tong et al., Woods et al. and Etienney et al. (cited in Q1).
- Lieb–Robinson-based bounds. Wang, Foss-Feig & Hazzard derive rigorous bounds on finite-size error, which they call "ubiquitous in numerical simulations of quantum many body systems", for dynamics from product states in locally interacting systems; periodic boundaries give smaller error than open ones at early times, and the bounds are checked on 1D Ising and Fermi–Hubbard simulations — [arXiv:2009.12032](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.12032); [PRR 3, L032047](https://journals.aps.org/prresearch/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevResearch.3.L032047). Haah, Hastings, Kothari & Low use locality to simulate lattice Hamiltonians with O(nT polylog(nT/ε)) gates — [arXiv:1801.03922](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.03922).
- A shipped estimator can itself be wrong: PennyLane's Trotter-error estimator "should add up expectation values of nested commutators, not states" (earlier report) — [PennyLane #8152](https://github.com/PennyLaneAI/pennylane/issues/8152).

#### Validated numerics (interval and ball arithmetic, stochastic arithmetic)
- CAPD::DynSys (Kapela, Mrozek, Wilczak & Zgliczyński; CNSNS 101, 2021) is a C++ library for rigorous integration of ODEs, rigorous Poincaré maps and variational equations, and differential inclusions, used in computer-assisted proofs — [CAPD citation page](http://capd.ii.uj.edu.pl/citation.php); [Recent advances in rigorous computation of Poincaré maps](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08046) (search summary).
- VNODE-LP (Nedialkov) computes an interval vector guaranteed to contain the solution of an ODE initial-value problem. It was written as a literate program so that "its correctness can be verified by a human expert", and is "generally suitable for computing bounds on the solution of an IVP ODE with point initial conditions, or interval initial conditions with a sufficiently small width, over not very long time intervals" — [VNODE-LP manual](https://www.cas.mcmaster.ca/~nedialk/vnodelp/doc/vnode.pdf) (search summary).
- Immler formally verified in Isabelle/HOL a rigorous reachability algorithm (adaptive Runge–Kutta with affine arithmetic) and used it to certify the computations in Tucker's proof of the Lorenz attractor — [A Verified ODE Solver and the Lorenz Attractor](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6044317/); [TACAS 2015](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-662-46681-0_3) (search summary).
- Matrix-exponential enclosures (Miyajima) are cited in Q1; CERTIFY-ED's 50-digit `mpmath` references are cited above.
- Stochastic arithmetic: Verificarlo is an LLVM pass applying Monte Carlo Arithmetic to C, C++ and Fortran; "unlike source-to-source approaches, our implementation captures the influence of compiler optimizations on the numerical accuracy" — [arXiv:1509.01347](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.01347). A search summary of the same paper reports that CADNA "sometimes over-estimate[s] result significant digits" — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/1509.01347) (search summary).

#### Formal verification that touches quantum computing
- VOQC (Hietala, Rand, Hung, Wu & Hicks; POPL 2021) is a circuit optimizer verified in Coq. Its z-rotation parameters are rational multiples of π ("rational, not float"), which sidesteps floating-point angle error — [VOQC paper](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~mwh/papers/voqc.pdf) (search summary).
- CoqQ (POPL 2023) is "the first formally verified program verifier for a high-level quantum language" (qwhile), with a Floyd–Hoare logic proved relatively complete — [arXiv:2207.11350](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.11350) (search summary).
- QBlue is a compiler for second-quantized Hamiltonian simulation whose type system "enforces Hermitian structure". It compiles to digital and analog circuits and is "fully mechanized in the Rocq proof framework", making it "the first end-to-end verified compiler for second-quantized Hamiltonian simulation" — [arXiv:2509.18583](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.18583). Its abstract does not claim verified numerical (Trotter or roundoff) error bounds.
- Gleipnir (Tao, Shi, Yao, Hui, Chong & Gu; PLDI 2021) computes verified error bounds for noisy quantum programs with a (ρ̂, δ)-diamond norm evaluated through MPS. For programs of 20 or more qubits its bounds are 15–30% tighter than unconstrained diamond norms, and at MPS width 128 it returns bounds within 6 minutes — [arXiv:2104.06349](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06349) (search summary).
- Verified classical ODE numerics. Kellison & Appel verify in Coq that a C program accurately solves an ODE initial-value problem, using a floating-point functional model and a real-valued model — [Verified Numerical Methods for ODEs](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-21222-2_9); [VerifiedLeapfrog (harmonic oscillator)](https://github.com/VeriNum/VerifiedLeapfrog). VCFloat2 automates floating-point error analysis in Coq — [VCFloat2](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~appel/papers/vcfloat2.pdf). A formally verified round-off analysis of Runge–Kutta methods also exists — [J. Autom. Reasoning 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10817-023-09686-y) (title via search summary).
- LLM-driven formalization of quantum mechanics: AxQM offers "1,019 kernel-checkable proof-synthesis tasks" from Nielsen & Chuang in a custom Lean library of finite-dimensional quantum mechanics, graded deterministically by the Lean kernel — [arXiv:2609.05157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05157).

### Inferences
- *Inference:* in V&V vocabulary, library test suites perform code verification with weak oracles. Solution verification (showing that a specific run converged in cutoff, time step, χ, trajectory count and precision) is left to users and rarely documented; ITensor says so explicitly, and the earlier report found no syllabus teaching it.
- *Inference:* test tolerances of 10⁻³ to 0.1 (Dynamiqs' 10⁻³ state error, QuTiP's 0.05 qubit test, QuantumOptics.jl's 0.1 trajectory test, QuantumToolbox.jl's 0.1–0.6 shifted-Fock tests) cannot detect errors of the size that matter for gate-fidelity work (10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁹), so passing suites certify very little at that scale.
- *Inference:* the rigorous toolkit splits into three groups.
  - Computable a posteriori quantities that could be wrapped around existing solvers today: Etienney et al., TimeEvolver, Woods et al.'s monitoring, Hubig et al.'s variance, and residuals.
  - A priori bounds still too loose for pass/fail use, though improving (Childs et al., Blunt et al.).
  - Validated and formal methods confined to small dimensions, short times, compilers or single simple integrators. No formally verified Lindblad or many-qubit Schrödinger solver was found.
- *Inference:* CERTIFY-ED is the closest precedent for an external verification layer around quantum numerics: multi-oracle, invariant layers, certificates and error injection. It covers static exact diagonalization only, and it is an early two-author preprint, so its claims should be treated as unreviewed.

### Gaps
- Oberkampf & Roy's text was not read; the code-vs-solution verification distinction rests on Roy (2005) and on titles (Q4).
- Nightly or long-running CI and benchmark repositories (e.g., `qutip-benchmark`) were not examined for accuracy checks beyond the unit tests.
- No source was found applying interval or validated ODE solvers to Lindblad or many-qubit Schrödinger dynamics (one search returned unrelated results).
- No test suites were read for QuTiP-QIP, Qiskit Aer, Cirq or PennyLane, so the claims here cover the six libraries named.

## 3. Documented incidents where numerical error produced wrong published or reported quantum results

### Takeaway
Clear published incidents are rare and hard to find. The strongest case is Nature's 2020 retraction of a 2016 quantum-control paper (Quantum Moves) over an error in its optimization code, found by outside researchers. The most consequential near-miss is IBM's 2023 utility experiment: the paper's MPS and isoTNS references could not validate the hard regime, and independent classical methods disagreed with each other by up to 20% before later work converged them. Issue trackers hold many reports of silently wrong numbers, but most are logic or semantics bugs (gate decompositions, gate fusion, step counting, conventions) rather than discretization or floating-point error. The numerical-analysis class (step control, truncation, eigen and steady-state solvers, precision, GPU linear algebra) is smaller but recurs across libraries. In both published cases the error was caught by outsiders reimplementing or recomputing, not by the authors' internal checks.

### Cited Findings
- **Quantum Moves (Nature 2016; retracted 2020).** The original paper reported that players of a quantum-control game outperformed numerical optimization — [Sørensen et al., Nature 2016](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature17620). Nature issued an Editorial Expression of Concern in May 2020 — [EEoC](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-2274-0) — and a retraction in July 2020. According to the retraction note, the authors retracted "owing to an error in their computer code that meant the quantitative results reported were not valid", an error pointed out by A. ### Inferences
- *Inference:* in both published-result cases (Quantum Moves; IBM utility), detection came from independent reimplementation or competing algorithms run by other groups, not from the original authors' checks. Independent recomputation is the verification channel that has actually worked at publication level.
- *Inference:* of the roughly 23 issues read in full this session (plus about 15 read by title), most root causes are logic, semantics or bookkeeping errors: gate decomposition, fusion, chunk counting, normalization policy, cached flags, conventions. Cheap generic checks would catch many of them, without physics expertise:
  - differential runs inside one library (fusion on/off, MPS vs statevector, master equation vs trajectory average, ODE vs exp(Lt));
  - invariant checks (variational bound, trace, Hermiticity);
  - bookkeeping assertions (steps taken vs steps requested).
- *Inference:* the cases that need domain knowledge are a minority, but they are the ones that change physics conclusions: wrong-limit truncation (Fischer et al.), non-normal relaxation (Mori & Shirai), modelling conventions (QuTiP #1719, #2828) and approximate contraction (BP, MPS at the entanglement frontier).

### Gaps
- No systematic errata search across APS, Nature, Quantum and IOP for "error in the numerical code" in quantum-simulation papers was completed: two phrase searches returned no matching errata, and the search budget then ran out. No curated database of quantum-numerics errata was found.
- The Nature retraction note could not be opened directly; its wording comes from a search summary.
- It was not established whether any published paper's conclusions depended on QuTiP pulse skipping, default cutoffs, or single-precision defaults.

## 4. Which general solver-verification techniques transfer to quantum numerics, and where does quantum structure break them?

### Takeaway
Most of the classical V&V toolkit transfers once it is adapted to quantum structure. Convergence and order-of-accuracy studies, differential and metamorphic testing, stochastic arithmetic and precision sweeps all apply directly at teaching and medium scale, where re-running a solver is cheap. What breaks are the pieces that assume a generic ODE or PDE:
- manufactured solutions add source terms that destroy unitarity and complete positivity, and many quantum integrators cannot accept such terms;
- observed-order tests fail for adaptive solvers, for unbounded generators, and in pre-asymptotic oscillatory regimes;
- interval methods hit the wrapping effect and exponential dimension;
- differential testing raises false alarms from conventions;
- truncation convergence can mislead without any numerical symptom.

Quantum-native substitutes exist or are easy to construct: structure-preserving manufactured problems through frame transformations, invariant and residual checks, quantum metamorphic relations, computable truncation and time-step bounds, and diamond-norm error metrics. LLM-based testing has so far found crash and API bugs in quantum SDKs; no evidence was found that it supplies numerical oracles.

### Cited Findings
- **Classical V&V framework.**
  - Roache's method of manufactured solutions (MMS) "provides a straightforward procedure for generating" exact benchmark solutions; combined with systematic grid refinement it produces "strong Code Verifications with a theorem-like quality and a clearly defined completion point". Order-of-accuracy verification compares the observed order with the formal order — [Roache 2002, J. Fluids Eng. 124(1):4–10](https://asmedigitalcollection.asme.org/fluidsengineering/article-abstract/124/1/4/462791/Code-Verification-by-the-Method-of-Manufactured) (abstract via search summary).
  - The Grid Convergence Index — [Roache 1994](https://doi.org/10.1115/1.2910291).
  - Code vs solution verification — [Roy 2005, "Review of code and solution verification procedures for computational simulation"](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcp.2004.10.036).
  - Textbook treatment — [Oberkampf & Roy 2010](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511760396); the 2025 second edition adds uncertainty quantification to the title — [CUP 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781009031004).
  - ASME V&V 20's scope is "the quantification of the degree of accuracy of simulation of specified validation variables at a specified validation point", using concepts from experimental uncertainty analysis. It defines error as a deficiency "not due to lack of knowledge" and uncertainty as one "due to lack of knowledge" — [ASME V&V 20](https://www.asme.org/codes-standards/find-codes-standards/standard-for-verification-and-validation-in-computational-fluid-dynamics-and-heat-transfer); [ANSI preview](https://webstore.ansi.org/preview-pages/asme/preview_vv-20_2009_r2016.pdf) (search summary).
  - MMS is routine in plasma and fluid codes, for example BOUT++ — [arXiv:1602.06747](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.06747) (title via search).
- **The oracle problem.** Kanewala & Bieman's systematic review lists the lack of an oracle as a defining challenge of scientific software and notes that "choosing suitable tolerances for an oracle when testing numerical programs is difficult due to the involvement of complex floating point computations". The mitigations they list include pseudo-oracles, analytical solutions and experimental results — [IST 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0950584914001232) (search summary).
- **Differential testing on quantum stacks.** QDiff (ASE 2021) generates semantically equivalent programs, compares output distributions with Kolmogorov–Smirnov and cross-entropy tests across Qiskit, Cirq and Pyquil on simulators and hardware, and found "several critical bugs" — [QDiff](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10342994-qdiff-differential-testing-quantum-software-stacks) (search summary).
- **Metamorphic testing.** MorphQ generated "over 8k program pairs within two days, many of which expose crashes", leading to 13 Qiskit bugs, nine of them confirmed — [arXiv:2206.01111](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.01111).
- **LLM-driven fuzzing.** Fuzz4All found 98 bugs across GCC, Clang, Z3, CVC5, OpenJDK and Qiskit, 64 of them confirmed as previously unknown — [arXiv:2308.04748](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.04748) (search summary). Of these, 11 were in Qiskit (8 confirmed), for example a crash in QASM export and import (search summary of the paper body).
- **LLM-generated oracles.** An ASE 2025 study reports that LLM-generated oracles raise mutation scores to levels comparable with human-written ones — [ASE 2025](https://conf.researchr.org/details/ase-2025/ase-2025-papers/10/Do-LLMs-Generate-Useful-Test-Oracles-An-Empirical-Study-with-an-Unbiased-Dataset) (search summary). Search summaries also mention trouble with arithmetic in LLM-generated assertions (source not opened). A 2025 research roadmap surveys LLM-based testing — [arXiv:2509.25043](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25043).
- **Floating-point analysis tools.** Verificarlo and CADNA (cited in Q2).
- **Where quantum structure breaks transfer (sources from Q1–Q3):**
  - *Complete positivity and trace.* Only matrix-exponential updates are CPTP among the common methods — [Riesch & Jirauschek](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.00416). CPTP-by-construction schemes take Kraus or channel form — [Appelö & Cheng](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08898); [Robin, Rouchon & Sellem](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01712).
  - *Unbounded generators.* Trotter order breaks down for some states — [Burgarth et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.08044). Truncation creates CFL-type stiffness — [Robin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01712). Truncation can converge to the wrong limit with no numerical symptom — [Fischer et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15889).
  - *Non-normality.* The Liouvillian gap does not set the relaxation time — [Mori & Shirai](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10953).
  - *Interval methods.* Suitable for "not very long time intervals" and narrow initial sets — [VNODE-LP](https://www.cas.mcmaster.ca/~nedialk/vnodelp/doc/vnode.pdf) (search summary). A sharp interval exponential is reportedly NP-hard — [arXiv:0908.3954](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.3954) (attribution unverified).
  - *Loose a priori bounds.* Product formulas are better judged by empirical estimates — [Childs et al. PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1801723115) (search summary). Triangle-inequality evaluation loosens bounds — [Blunt et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11621).
  - *Conventions.* [QuTiP #1719](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/1719); [QuTiP #2828](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/issues/2828); global phase in [Qiskit Aer #977](https://github.com/Qiskit/qiskit-aer/issues/977).
  - *Precision-blind tolerances.* [Cirq #5268](https://github.com/quantumlib/Cirq/issues/5268); [Dynamiqs sharp bits](https://www.dynamiqs.org/stable/documentation/getting_started/sharp-bits.html).
  - *Cosmetic repair of invariants.* QuTiP's `normalize_output` — [mesolve.py](https://github.com/qutip/qutip/blob/56ed7854177e046124118fdcb6b958d557b65984/qutip/solver/mesolve.py#L110-L111).
- **Quantum-native verification metrics and layers.**
  - Gleipnir's (ρ̂, δ)-diamond norm — [arXiv:2104.06349](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06349) (search summary).
  - CERTIFY-ED's layers (algebraic invariants, analytic limits, alternative algorithms, arbitrary precision, conservation laws, dynamical consistency, finite-size scaling) and its error-injection self-test — [arXiv:2605.11787](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11787).

### Inferences
- *Transfer table (inference, built from the cited findings):*

| Technique | Transfers? | Quantum adaptation | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manufactured solutions (MMS) | Partly | Use frame-transformation problems instead of source terms: pick a unitary U(t) and set H(t) = iU̇U† so that ψ(t) = U(t)ψ₀ is exact and Hermiticity holds; for Lindblad, conjugate a solvable Lindbladian by a time-dependent unitary, which keeps the problem CPTP with a known solution (no source found that does this; untested idea) | Source-term MMS breaks unitarity and CP and so tests a different equation; exponential, splitting and Kraus integrators often cannot take an inhomogeneous term |
| Order-of-accuracy tests | Yes for fixed-step methods (Trotter, Magnus, RK, SDE as in QuTiP) | Measure error in physical norms (trace distance, infidelity); for adaptive solvers test tolerance proportionality instead of order | Unbounded generators (order reduction, Burgarth et al.); pre-asymptotic oscillatory regime (dt not ≪ 1/‖H‖); adaptive step control |
| Convergence studies, Richardson extrapolation, GCI | Yes | Treat cutoff, dt, bond dimension χ, trajectory count and precision as "grids"; extrapolate in discarded weight or variance (Hubig) and in Trotter step (Rendon) | Wrong-limit convergence (Fischer et al.); non-monotone convergence in χ near the entanglement frontier; cost grows exponentially with system size |
| Differential testing | Yes | Normalize conventions; compare density matrices, physical observables, eigenspaces as projectors, states up to global phase | Conventions (QuTiP #1719, #2828; Aer #977); common-mode failures when tools share LAPACK, SciPy ODE codes or default tolerances |
| Metamorphic testing | Yes; the best fit | Loschmidt echo (H → −H returns ψ₀); unitary frame changes; symmetry sectors and conserved quantities; qubit relabelling; time and energy rescaling (Dynamiqs advises choosing units); Kraus-representation freedom; trajectory average = master equation | Relations hold exactly only in exact arithmetic, so tolerances must be derived from solver settings; frame changes also change stiffness |
| Floating-point analysis (stochastic arithmetic, precision sweeps) | Yes at kernel level | Run precision sweeps (complex64 → complex128 → extended); compute infidelity without cancellation; watch exponent range in contractions | Large tensor contractions (exponent range); GPU fp32 defaults |
| Interval and validated numerics | Small d only | Matrix-exponential enclosures (Miyajima); arbitrary-precision references (CERTIFY-ED) | Wrapping effect, long oscillatory times, exponential dimension |
| Formal verification | Compilers and simple integrators | VOQC, CoqQ, QBlue for semantics; Gleipnir for noise bounds; Coq-verified leapfrog for an oscillator | No formally verified Lindblad or many-qubit Schrödinger solver found |
| LLM-generated tests | For API and crash bugs (Fuzz4All, Qiskit) | Have the LLM write invariant checks, metamorphic relations and convergence harnesses rather than expected numbers | Oracle problem: an LLM cannot know the right number or a defensible tolerance |

- *Inference: what an external numerical-solver verification product could realistically catch in quantum simulation, ordered by yield per effort:*
  1. Step-control failures (skipped pulses, imprecise click times, step-limit stalls), caught by automated tolerance, `max_step` and breakpoint sweeps plus instrumentation of right-hand-side evaluation times.
  2. Precision artefacts (fp32 defaults on GPUs and in Dynamiqs, fidelity cancellation, trace decay), caught by precision sweeps and cancellation-free metric formulations.
  3. Invariant violations hidden by library repairs, caught by recomputing norm, trace, Hermiticity, positivity and the Choi-matrix CPTP conditions from raw output with renormalization off and tolerances scaled to precision.
  4. Logic bugs that show up as disagreement between methods (fusion on/off, MPS vs statevector, trajectory average vs master equation, ODE vs exp(Lt), steady state vs long-time evolution).
  5. Truncation and bond-dimension non-convergence, caught by sweeps with tail-weight and discarded-weight accounting, and, for Lindblad with Fock truncation, by implementing the computable bounds of Etienney et al.
  6. Statistical errors in trajectories, caught by confidence intervals, 1/√N scaling and seed-stability checks.
- *Inference: what such a product could not catch without domain modelling or analysis:* basis-dependent wrong limits (Fischer et al.); modelling approximations (rotating-wave approximation, Kerr approximation of a transmon, Pauli twirling, Markov approximation), which are validation questions rather than verification; convention mismatches, which need a declared physical specification; and new physics with no reference. Rigorous guarantees also stay out of reach at scale, because interval enclosures and formal proofs do not reach large Hilbert spaces.
- *Inference: market fit.* The earlier report found that no incumbent publishes error against a converged reference and that every teaching-scale problem finishes in seconds. Re-running 10–100 times under perturbed settings is therefore affordable exactly where students and small groups work, and a harness that wraps QuTiP, Dynamiqs or QuantumToolbox.jl calls and emits a convergence and invariant certificate would cover items 1–6 without changing the solvers. CERTIFY-ED shows the same certificate pattern for exact diagonalization.

### Gaps
- No source was found applying MMS to Lindblad or many-body Schrödinger solvers. The frame-transformation construction above is an untested proposal from this note.
- No study was found that measures LLM-generated tests or oracles on numerical scientific code with tolerance-based oracles, quantum or otherwise.
- The ASME V&V 20 standard and Oberkampf & Roy were not read directly; definitions come from abstracts, previews and titles.
- No primary source was found on tolerance-proportionality testing of adaptive quantum solvers.
