### Stern-Gerlach magnets in sequence

A beam of spin-$j$ atoms meets three Stern-Gerlach magnets. The first, along $z$, lets only its top beam $m=j$ through; the second, tilted by $\theta$ from $z$, again lets only its top beam through; the third, along $z$, splits what arrives into its $2j+1$ beams. What fraction of the atoms that leave the first magnet comes out of each beam of the third?

With $\hbar=1$, a magnet along $\hat n$ projects onto the eigenstates $|\hat n,m\rangle$ of $\hat n\cdot\mathbf J$. Rotating the whole setup about $z$ puts the second axis at $\hat n=(\sin\theta,0,\cos\theta)$, so $|\hat n,j\rangle=e^{-i\theta J_y}|z,j\rangle$, and Born's rule applied twice gives

$$
P(m)=\left|\langle z,j|\hat n,j\rangle\right|^2\left|\langle z,m|\hat n,j\rangle\right|^2=\left|d^{\,j}_{jj}(\theta)\right|^2\left|d^{\,j}_{mj}(\theta)\right|^2,\qquad d^{\,j}_{m'm}(\theta)=\langle z,m'|e^{-i\theta J_y}|z,m\rangle .
$$

QF rotates $|z,j\rangle$ by the Wigner rotation, with $\theta$ kept as a symbol, for $j=1/2$, $1$ and $3/2$:

```wl
sgFractions[j_] := With[{amps = QuantumOperator["WignerD"[j, θ]][QuantumState[UnitVector[2 j + 1, 2 j + 1], 2 j + 1]]["AmplitudesList"]},
   FullSimplify[Abs[Last[amps]]^2 Abs[amps]^2, θ ∈ Reals]];
sgPanel[j_] := With[{fr = sgFractions[j]},
   Plot[Evaluate[Append[Reverse[fr], Total[fr]]], {θ, 0, π}, PlotRange -> All, PlotStyle -> Append[Table[Automatic, 2 j + 1], Dashed],
    PlotLegends -> Placed[Append[Table[Row[{"m = ", m}], {m, j, -j, -1}], "passes magnet 2"], Below],
    Frame -> True, FrameLabel -> {"θ", "fraction of atoms"}, FrameTicks -> {{Automatic, None}, {Range[0, π, π/4], None}},
    PlotLabel -> Style[Row[{"j = ", j, ":   P(m = \[Minus]j) = ", TraditionalForm[First[fr]]}], 13, FontFamily -> "Helvetica"], ImageSize -> 330]];
Grid[{sgPanel /@ {1/2, 1, 3/2}}, Alignment -> Top]
```

With the second magnet aligned with the first, every atom stays in the top beam. Tilting it refills beams the first magnet had emptied, the bottom beam included, even though the second magnet only removes atoms. The bottom beam is largest when the middle magnet is perpendicular, and it is weaker for larger spin.

### Compiling a rotation from H and T gates

A fault-tolerant qubit offers only a finite gate set: the Clifford gates generated by $H$ and $S=T^2$, which are cheap, and the $T$ gate, which is expensive. Any target rotation $U$ can only be approximated by a product of them. How fast does the best approximation improve as the number of $T$ gates grows?

With $T=\mathrm{diag}(1,e^{i\pi/4})$ and $S=T^2$, every product of these gates equals, up to a global phase, a normal form (Matsumoto and Amano)

$$
W=\left(T\ \text{or}\ 1\right)\,s_1s_2\cdots s_k\,C,\qquad s_i\in\{HT,\ SHT\},\qquad C\in\text{the 24 Cliffords},
$$

whose $T$ count is $k$ or $k+1$, so from $T$ count 1 on, each extra $T$ gate doubles the number of candidates. The error of $W$ is $\epsilon=1-\tfrac12|\mathrm{Tr}\,W^\dagger U|$, which ignores the global phase.

The code enumerates every normal form up to $T$ count 16 and keeps the smallest error, for three rotations $R_{\hat n}(\alpha)=e^{-i\alpha\,\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2}$ ($R_{x+z}$ rotates about $(\hat x+\hat z)/\sqrt2$); QF draws the circuit, in time order, that gives the $R_z(0.3)$ point at $T$ count 7:

```wl
{ctH, ctS, ctT} = N[{HadamardMatrix[2], DiagonalMatrix[{1, I}], DiagonalMatrix[{1, Exp[I π/4]}]}];
ctMatrix[w_] := Fold[#2 . #1 &, IdentityMatrix[2], w /. {"H" -> ctH, "S" -> ctS}];
ctCliffords = DeleteDuplicatesBy[Catenate[Table[Tuples[{"H", "S"}, l], {l, 0, 6}]],
   With[{f = Flatten[ctMatrix[#]]}, Round[f Exp[-I Arg[SelectFirst[f, Abs[#] > 0.1 &]]], 10.^-6]] &];
ctSyllables = NestList[Join[# . ctH . ctT, # . ctS . ctH . ctT] &, {IdentityMatrix[2]}, 16];
ctOverlaps[t_, u_] := Abs[Flatten[Conjugate[If[t == 0, ctSyllables[[1]], Join[ctSyllables[[t + 1]], ctT . # & /@ ctSyllables[[t]]]]], {{1}, {2, 3}}] .
     Transpose[Flatten[u . ConjugateTranspose[ctMatrix[#]]] & /@ ctCliffords]]/2;
ctTargets = <|Row[{Subscript["R", "x+z"], "(0.8)"}] -> MatrixExp[-0.4 I (PauliMatrix[1] + PauliMatrix[3])/Sqrt[2]],
   Row[{Subscript["R", "z"], "(0.3)"}] -> MatrixExp[-0.15 I PauliMatrix[3]], Row[{Subscript["R", "x"], "(1)"}] -> MatrixExp[-0.5 I PauliMatrix[1]]|>;
ctBestWord[t_, u_] := With[{pos = First[Position[#, Max[#]]] &[ctOverlaps[t, u]]},
   Join[ctCliffords[[pos[[2]]]], Catenate[IntegerDigits[pos[[1]] - 1 - If[pos[[1]] > 2^t, 2^t, 0], 2, t - Boole[pos[[1]] > 2^t]] /. {0 -> {"T", "H"}, 1 -> {"T", "H", "S"}}],
    If[pos[[1]] > 2^t, {"T"}, {}]]];
Column[{ListLogPlot[1 - FoldList[Max, Table[Max[ctOverlaps[t, #]], {t, 0, 16}]] & /@ Values[ctTargets], DataRange -> {0, 16}, Joined -> True,
    PlotMarkers -> Automatic, PlotLegends -> Keys[ctTargets], Frame -> True, FrameLabel -> {"T count", "best error ε"}, PlotLabel -> Row[{"normal forms over ", Length[ctCliffords], " Cliffords"}], ImageSize -> 450],
  QuantumCircuitOperator[ctBestWord[7, ctTargets[[2]]]]["Diagram", ImageSize -> 650]}, Alignment -> Center]
```

The best error falls roughly exponentially in the $T$ count, but in steps: a target can sit far from every circuit of one $T$ count and close to one of the next. $R_{x+z}(0.8)$ happens to lie close to a circuit with three $T$ gates that no circuit up to $T$ count 16 improves on.

### Phase estimation: how many qubits resolve two eigenphases

A one-qubit unitary has the eigenphases $\varphi_1$ and $\varphi_2=\varphi_1+\delta$, and phase estimation is run with $t$ control qubits on a target that is an equal superposition of the two eigenvectors. How many control qubits separate the two phases in the measured distribution?

With $U|u_k\rangle=e^{2\pi i\varphi_k}|u_k\rangle$, the controls in $|+\rangle^{\otimes t}$ pick up the phases of $U^{2^{t-1}},\dots,U^2,U$ and an inverse Fourier transform reads them out. Each eigenvector contributes with its weight, so the outcome $x$ appears with probability

$$
P(x)=\sum_{k=1,2}\frac{|c_k|^2}{4^t}\left|\sum_{y=0}^{2^t-1}e^{2\pi i y(\varphi_k-x/2^t)}\right|^2,\qquad |c_1|^2=|c_2|^2=\tfrac12 ,
$$

a peak of width about $2^{-t}$ around each $\varphi_k$.

QF runs its phase-estimation circuit on $U=\mathrm{diag}(e^{2\pi i\varphi_1},e^{2\pi i\varphi_2})$ with $\varphi_1=0.17$, its initial $X$ on the target removed and the target in $|+\rangle$; dashed lines mark the two eigenphases:

```wl
qpeProbs[t_, δ_] := N[QuantumCircuitOperator["PhaseEstimation"[QuantumOperator[DiagonalMatrix[Exp[2 π I {0.17, 0.17 + δ}]]], t]][[2 ;;]]][
    QuantumTensorProduct[QuantumState[StringRepeat["0", t]], QuantumState["+"]]]["ProbabilitiesList"];
qpePanel[δ_, t_] := ListPlot[Transpose[{Range[0, 2^t - 1]/2^t, qpeProbs[t, δ]}], Filling -> Axis, PlotRange -> {{0, 0.5}, {0, 0.75}},
   GridLines -> {{0.17, 0.17 + δ}, None}, GridLinesStyle -> Dashed, Frame -> True, FrameLabel -> {Row[{"x / ", Superscript[2, "t"]}], "P(x)"},
   PlotLabel -> Row[{"δ = ", δ, ",   t = ", t}], ImageSize -> 300];
Grid[Outer[qpePanel, {0.1, 0.02}, {3, 5, 7}]]
```

The two peaks merge while $2^{-t}$ is larger than $\delta$ and separate once it is a few times smaller, so a smaller separation needs more control qubits. A phase that falls between grid points $x/2^t$ spreads its probability over the nearest bars.

### Shor's algorithm factors 15

To factor $N=15$, pick $a$ coprime to 15 and find its order $r$, the smallest $r$ with $a^r\equiv1 \pmod{15}$. If $r$ is even and $a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1$, then $\gcd(a^{r/2}\pm1,15)$ are factors. Can phase estimation find $r$ from a single distribution?

The work register holds $y\in\{0,\dots,14\}$ in four qubits, and the multiplier $U_a|y\rangle=|ay\bmod 15\rangle$ (identity on $y=15$) is unitary because $a$ is invertible mod 15. Starting from $|1\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt r}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}|u_k\rangle$, an equal superposition of eigenvectors with $U_a|u_k\rangle=e^{2\pi ik/r}|u_k\rangle$, phase estimation with $t=8$ control qubits returns $x$ with $x/2^t\approx k/r$, each $k$ with probability $1/r$. Here $r$ divides $2^8$, so $x/2^t=k/r$ exactly and the order is the least common multiple of the denominators of $x/2^t$.

QF runs the 12-qubit phase-estimation circuit for six values of $a$, its initial $X$ gates removed and the work register in $|1\rangle=|0001\rangle$; each title gives the order read from the peaks and the two greatest common divisors:

```wl
shorU[a_] := QuantumOperator[SparseArray[Append[Thread[Transpose[{Mod[a Range[0, 14], 15], Range[0, 14]}] + 1 -> 1], {16, 16} -> 1]]];
shorProbs[a_] := N[QuantumCircuitOperator["PhaseEstimation"[shorU[a], 8]][[5 ;;]]][
    QuantumTensorProduct[QuantumState[StringRepeat["0", 8]], QuantumState["0001"]]]["ProbabilitiesList"];
shor15Panel[a_] := With[{p = shorProbs[a]}, With[{r = LCM @@ Denominator[(Flatten[Position[p, _?(# > 10^-6 &)]] - 1)/2^8]},
    ListPlot[Select[Transpose[{Range[0, 255]/256, p}], Last[#] > 10^-6 &], Filling -> Axis, PlotRange -> {{-0.03, 1}, {0, 0.55}}, Frame -> True,
     FrameLabel -> {Row[{"x / ", Superscript[2, 8]}], "P(x)"}, FrameTicks -> {{Automatic, None}, {Range[0, 1, 1/4], None}},
     PlotLabel -> Row[{"a = ", a, ",   r = ", r, ",   gcd = ", GCD[a^(r/2) - 1, 15], ", ", GCD[a^(r/2) + 1, 15]}], ImageSize -> 300]]];
Grid[Partition[shor15Panel /@ {2, 4, 7, 11, 13, 14}, 3]]
```

Every distribution is $r$ equal peaks at the multiples of $1/r$, so the denominators give the order at once. Five of the six choices of $a$ give the factors; for $a=14\equiv-1$ the order is even but $a^{r/2}\equiv-1$, the divisors are trivial, and the algorithm must restart with another $a$.

### One oracle query: Deutsch-Jozsa and Bernstein-Vazirani

An oracle evaluates a Boolean function $f$ of $n$ bits. Deutsch-Jozsa is promised that $f$ is constant or balanced (zero on exactly half of the inputs) and must say which; Bernstein-Vazirani is promised $f(x)=s\cdot x \bmod 2$ and must find the string $s$. Does a single query settle each problem?

Both algorithms query the phase oracle $O_f|x\rangle=(-1)^{f(x)}|x\rangle$ once on $|+\rangle^{\otimes n}$ and measure each qubit in the $X$ basis, which is $H^{\otimes n}$ followed by a computational-basis measurement; QF's Bernstein-Vazirani circuit realizes the oracle with CNOTs onto an extra qubit in $|-\rangle$. The outcome $z$ appears with probability

$$
P(z)=\Bigl|\,2^{-n}\sum_{x}(-1)^{f(x)+z\cdot x}\Bigr|^2,\qquad P(0)=\Bigl(1-\frac{2w}{2^n}\Bigr)^2,\qquad w=\#\{x: f(x)=1\},
$$

and for $f(x)=s\cdot x$ the sum is $2^n\delta_{z,s}$.

QF runs its Deutsch-Jozsa circuit on up to four random three-bit functions of each weight $w$ (points, offset sideways; the dashed curve is $P(0)$ above), and its Bernstein-Vazirani circuit on all sixteen four-bit secrets:

```wl
djZeroProb[k_] := First[QuantumCircuitOperator["DeutschJozsaPhase"[BooleanFunction[k, 3]]][]["ProbabilitiesList"]];
djPoints = BlockRandom[SeedRandom[3]; Catenate[KeyValueMap[Function[{w, ks}, With[{sample = RandomSample[ks, UpTo[4]]},
       MapIndexed[{w + 0.12 (First[#2] - (Length[sample] + 1)/2), djZeroProb[#1]} &, sample]]],
     KeySort[GroupBy[Range[0, 255], DigitCount[#, 2, 1] &]]]]];
bvTable = Table[QuantumCircuitOperator["BernsteinVazirani"[IntegerString[s, 2, 4]]][]["ProbabilitiesList"], {s, 0, 15}];
Grid[{{Show[ListPlot[djPoints, PlotMarkers -> Automatic], Plot[(1 - w/4)^2, {w, 0, 8}, PlotStyle -> Dashed], PlotRange -> {{-0.3, 8.3}, {-0.05, 1.05}},
    Frame -> True, FrameTicks -> {{Automatic, None}, {Range[0, 8], None}}, FrameLabel -> {"weight w", "P(z = 000)"}, PlotLabel -> "Deutsch-Jozsa, n = 3", ImageSize -> 360],
   ArrayPlot[bvTable, Mesh -> All, Frame -> True, FrameLabel -> {"secret s", "outcome z"},
    FrameTicks -> {{Thread[{Range[1, 16, 3], Range[0, 15, 3]}], None}, {Thread[{Range[1, 16, 3], Range[0, 15, 3]}], None}},
    PlotLabel -> "Bernstein-Vazirani, n = 4: P(z | s), black = 1, white = 0", ImageSize -> 360]}}, Alignment -> Center]
```

The constant functions return all zeros with certainty and the balanced ones never do, so under the promise one query decides. Every other function lands on the curve in between, where one query cannot decide. For every secret, the Bernstein-Vazirani outcome is $s$ itself with probability 1.

### Gleason's theorem needs three outcomes

Gleason's theorem says that in dimension three or more the only rule that assigns probabilities to measurement outcomes, adding to 1 over every measurement, is the Born rule $\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle$; Busch's theorem extends this to a qubit once generalized measurements (POVMs) are allowed. Which measurements expose a rule that is not Born's?

Take the rule that replaces each Born probability $p$ by $g(p)=3p^2-2p^3$, which is increasing and maps $[0,1]$ onto itself. A qubit basis has two outcomes, $p$ and $1-p$; a qutrit basis, or the qubit trine POVM with effects $E_k=\frac13\left(1+\hat n_k\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\right)$ and $\hat n_k$ at $120^\circ$ in the $xz$ plane, has three, $u$, $v$ and $w=1-u-v$. The rule is consistent only where

$$
g(p)+g(1-p)=1\quad\text{(two outcomes)},\qquad g(u)+g(v)+g(w)=1\quad\text{(three outcomes)}.
$$

The code simplifies both sums and plots the three-outcome sum over the triangle of probabilities; QF supplies the outcome probabilities of the trine on twelve pure qubit states with Bloch vectors in the $xz$ plane and of a qutrit basis on twelve random pure states:

```wl
gleasonG[s_] := 3 s^2 - 2 s^3;
gleasonEffects = Table[(IdentityMatrix[2] + {Sin[2 π k/3], 0, Cos[2 π k/3]} . PauliMatrix[{1, 2, 3}])/3, {k, 0, 2}];
gleasonTrine = Table[Most[QuantumMeasurementOperator[gleasonEffects][QuantumState["BlochVector"[{Sin[φ], 0, Cos[φ]}]]]["ProbabilitiesList"]], {φ, 0, 11 π/6, π/6}];
gleasonQutrit = BlockRandom[SeedRandom[5]; Table[Most[QuantumState["RandomPure", 3]["ProbabilitiesList"]], 12]];
Grid[{{Plot[{gleasonG[p], p}, {p, 0, 1}, PlotStyle -> {Automatic, Dashed}, PlotLegends -> Placed[{"g(p)", "Born: p"}, Below],
    Frame -> True, FrameLabel -> {"Born probability p", "assigned probability"}, ImageSize -> 330,
    PlotLabel -> Style[Row[{"two outcomes: g(p) + g(1\[Minus]p) = ", Simplify[gleasonG[p] + gleasonG[1 - p]]}], 13, FontFamily -> "Helvetica"]],
   Show[DensityPlot[gleasonG[u] + gleasonG[v] + gleasonG[1 - u - v], {u, 0, 1}, {v, 0, 1}, RegionFunction -> (#1 + #2 <= 1 &), PlotLegends -> Automatic],
    ListPlot[{gleasonTrine, gleasonQutrit}, PlotMarkers -> {"\[FilledCircle]", "\[FilledSquare]"}, PlotLegends -> Placed[{"qubit trine POVM", "qutrit basis"}, Below]],
    Frame -> True, FrameLabel -> {"u", "v"}, AspectRatio -> 1, ImageSize -> 400,
    PlotLabel -> Style[Row[{"three outcomes: g(u) + g(v) + g(w) = 1 \[Minus] ", Simplify[(1 - gleasonG[u] - gleasonG[v] - gleasonG[1 - u - v])/(u v (1 - u - v))], " u v w"}], 13, FontFamily -> "Helvetica"]]}}, Alignment -> Top]
```

For two outcomes the sum is 1 identically, so a qubit basis cannot tell $g$ from the Born rule. For three outcomes the sum falls below 1 everywhere inside the triangle and reaches 1 only on its edges, where one outcome is impossible. The qutrit basis and the qubit trine put most states inside, so either measurement rules $g$ out.
