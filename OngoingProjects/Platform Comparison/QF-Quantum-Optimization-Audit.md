## 1. What people actually ask of quantum optimization

The questions below are ordered by how little a user must already believe for the question to arise: the first needs no commitment to any algorithm, the last presupposes the whole research program. The framing follows the field's own survey ([Abbas et al., Nature Reviews Physics 6, 718 (2024)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42254-024-00770-9), [arXiv:2312.02279](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.02279)) and the benchmark library the same community built to make the questions concrete ([QOBLIB, arXiv:2504.03832](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.03832)).

**Q1. State my problem, then put it in quantum form.** A user arrives with decision variables (yes/no choices, integers, sometimes continuous quantities), an objective, and constraints. Before any quantum step, this must become a quadratic function of binary variables with no constraints, because that is the only thing a cost Hamiltonian encodes directly: integers get split into bits, inequalities become equalities with slack bits, and each constraint is folded into the objective as a penalty term with a weight the user must choose. The result is a QUBO (quadratic unconstrained binary optimization) problem, and substituting each bit $x_i = (1-z_i)/2$ turns it into an Ising Hamiltonian, a sum of $Z$ and $ZZ$ terms whose ground state is the optimal assignment. Every quantum route (gate-based, annealing, Grover-type) starts here, and getting the penalty weights and encodings right is where most practical failures happen.

**Q2. Solve a combinatorial problem approximately, and tell me how good the answer is.** The canonical instances are graph problems (maximum cut, independent set, coloring), assignment and routing problems, portfolio selection, satisfiability. QOBLIB's ten problem classes, from market splitting to sports-league scheduling, are the community's current statement of "these are the problems that matter and resist classical solvers at moderate size." The user needs three things beyond a solver: the decoded answer in their own variables (not a bitstring), the objective value it achieves, and the gap to the best known or provably optimal value.

**Q3. Run the standard variational algorithms competently.** For QAOA (the quantum approximate optimization algorithm of [Farhi et al., arXiv:1411.4028](https://arxiv.org/abs/1411.4028)) this means: build the phase-separation layer $e^{-i\gamma H_C}$ from the cost Hamiltonian and the mixing layer $e^{-i\beta H_M}$ from a mixer, stack $p$ of each, and optimize the $2p$ angles. The literature's practical questions are parameter initialization and transfer (good angles concentrate and transfer between instances), depth choice, and above all constraint handling: penalties waste the Hilbert space on infeasible states, so mixers that never leave the feasible set (the XY mixer on one-hot registers, bit-flip mixers respecting independence constraints; [Hadfield et al., arXiv:1709.03489](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.03489), [arXiv:2203.06095](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.06095)) and warm starts from a classical solution are now standard practice. For VQE the questions are ansatz families and gradients: the parameter-shift rule gives exact derivatives from expectation values, and trainability (barren plateaus) decides whether any of it converges. The QAOA variant zoo is surveyed in [Blekos et al., Physics Reports (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09198).

**Q4. Find ground states of quantum Hamiltonians.** Chemistry and materials problems bring a Hamiltonian of fermionic creation and annihilation operators, $\sum h_{ij} c_i^\dagger c_j + \sum V_{ijkl} c_i^\dagger c_j^\dagger c_k c_l$. The needed machinery is the map from those operators to qubit operators (Jordan-Wigner and its relatives) plus physically motivated ansatz circuits. This is VQE's original use case and the reason every serious platform ships a fermion-to-qubit layer.

**Q5. Analyze the annealing route.** Adiabatic computation interpolates $H(s) = (1-s)H_B + sH_P$ and rides the instantaneous ground state. The user's questions: where is the minimum spectral gap, how does it scale, how long must the sweep be, what does a nonstandard schedule buy, and what do the actual dynamics do at finite time (diabatic transitions, reverse annealing). Note the structural fact that dominates this analysis in practice: for a cost Hamiltonian built only of $Z$ and $ZZ$ terms, flipping every spin is a symmetry, so the final ground state is always at least doubly degenerate and the physically relevant gap is to the first state *outside* the degenerate solution space, resolved within a symmetry sector.

**Q6. Is any of this better than classical?** The central open question of the field, and the one the surveys insist every tool must help answer rather than obscure: run the classical exact solver or the strong classical heuristic on the same instance, report approximation ratio and time-to-target against it, and treat the quantum result as a data point in that comparison. A tool that cannot produce the classical baseline next to the quantum answer invites self-deception.

**Q7. Understand the theory exactly.** Small-instance closed forms of QAOA landscapes, symmetry and degeneracy analysis, exact parameter concentration statements, the geometry of variational state space (the Fubini-Study metric and natural gradients), and the provable-speedup subroutines (Grover-type search and amplitude amplification, quantum linear-system solvers as components inside larger algorithms). This is the smallest audience but the one where exact computation, rather than floating-point sampling, is the actual requirement.

### 2.1 Verified behavior (live kernel, this machine)

**F1. The whole layer is invisible after loading the framework.** ``Needs["Wolfram`QuantumFramework`"]`` leaves the optimization context off `$ContextPath`; every name above then resolves in `` Global` `` with zero definitions. `QuantumLinearSolve[m, b]` returns unevaluated, `ParametrizedLayer[...]` sits inert inside a circuit spec, and nothing errors. The `PacletInfo.wl` `Symbols` list (26 entries) contains no optimization symbol, so this is not an oversight of one name but the contract for all fifteen. A user who has not read the tutorial's first cell concludes the feature does not exist.

**F2. Parameter names are captured from the user's session.** `ParametrizedLayer` and `GenerateParameters` build parameters with `Symbol["θ" <> ToString[i]]`. If the session already has `θ1 = 0.77`, then `ParametrizedLayer["RY", Range[2]]` silently emits `"RY"[0.77] -> {1}`: the ansatz arrives with one parameter already frozen to a leftover value, no message, and the optimizer happily optimizes the remaining ones. Verified live. The same mechanism means two circuits built in one session share parameter identities whether or not that was intended. The kernel's own convention for exactly this situation is formal symbols (`\[FormalTheta]`; `CoherentState` uses `\[FormalAlpha]`), which cannot carry values.

**F3. The natural-gradient step contradicts its own documentation on the singular case.** The tutorial and the update rule it prints promise the metric's pseudo-inverse, $\theta \to \theta - \eta\, g^{+}\nabla f$. The code calls `LinearSolve[metric, grad]` (`:645`, `:647`). On a barren-plateau direction the Fubini-Study metric is exactly singular (a parameter the state does not depend on gives a zero row). Verified live: with metric $\mathrm{diag}(1, 0)$ the iteration proceeds only because the gradient's second component is exactly zero; any inconsistent component (finite-difference noise on a flat direction is enough) makes `LinearSolve` fail inside `NestList`, and the trajectory fills with unevaluated expressions; verified directly, a cost carrying a $10^{-6}$ gradient on the flat direction makes the function itself emit `LinearSolve::nosol` twice and return a non-numeric trajectory. There is also no convergence test: both descent functions run exactly `"MaxIterations"` steps and return the whole trajectory. A second failure sits in the shared finite-difference gradient: on a cost that does not depend on one of its parameters at all, `CheapGradient` returns an unevaluated `centralFiniteDifference` expression (its `f_[vars__]` pattern cannot match a bare number), and the iteration doubles that held expression each step until the kernel runs out of memory (observed killed at 2.3 GB).

**F4. The adiabatic analyzer rejects the problem class it exists for.** `QuantumAdiabaticEvolve` computes the full symbolic eigensystem, sorts, and declares failure (message `::gap`, result `$Failed`) whenever the two lowest levels touch anywhere in $s \in [0,1]$ (`adiabaticTest`, `:169-184`). For any cost Hamiltonian built of $Z$/$ZZ$ terms alone, the global spin flip is a symmetry and the final ground state is doubly degenerate, so the gap closes at $s = 1$ by construction. Verified live: the three-qubit triangle Max-Cut interpolation runs 37 s of symbolic algebra and returns `$Failed`. The tool therefore handles textbook one- and two-qubit illustrations (1.3 s and 4.6 s live, matching the tutorial's closed forms) but not one genuine Ising optimization instance. The physically correct object is the gap within the symmetry sector of the initial state, or equivalently the gap to the first level outside the final solution space. Cost scaling is also intrinsically harsh: the full symbolic eigensystem of a $2^n$-dimensional interpolated Hamiltonian is Root-object algebra by $n = 2$ already (the tutorial shows this), and each analysis re-derives the whole spectrum even though only two levels enter the answer.

**F5. The linear solver is a demonstration, not a solver, and its cost says so.** With the default ansatz, `QuantumLinearSolve` optimizes one real amplitude per basis state ($2^n$ parameters, twice that when $b$ is complex). The circuit is contracted once, symbolically over the parametric ansatz, during setup (possible only because the amplitude ansatz is linear in its parameters; a genuine circuit ansatz would hit the symbolic growth of F6); each cost call then substitutes parameter values into the contracted state, a few milliseconds per call. Measured on this machine, default options, against the exact answer: 16 s for a $4\times4$ system, 31 s for $8\times8$, 110 s for $16\times16$ (about 60 s of it in the phase that builds the 256-term Pauli multiplexer circuit and the rest of the setup), with final answers agreeing with `LinearSolve` to between $10^{-9}$ and $10^{-5}$ depending on the run (`NMinimize`'s default method is stochastic, and on some draws the function's own `QuantumLinearSolve::error` global-phase warning fires), while `LinearSolve` itself takes a few microseconds on each. Nothing about the construction can scale: the ansatz has as many parameters as the vector has entries, so the method is classical least-squares routed through a quantum circuit. As pedagogy the multiplexer encoding is genuinely nice (it demonstrates coherent application of a non-unitary matrix); presented as a numerical method it invites the obvious dismissal. Two internal details reinforce that this is demonstration code relocated into the kernel: the function twice resets the kernel-global `$ModuleNumber` (`:773`, `:801`) so its generated parameter names look tidy, a session-wide side effect no library function should have, and its progress display constructs front-end objects inside the compute path (they happen to evaluate headless, verified, but stream a progress line to stdout every ~300 ms in scripts).

**F6. The symbolic-cost workflow, the framework's genuinely distinctive one, hits its wall at six qubits and is nowhere packaged.** The pattern the tutorial teaches (build a parametric circuit, take `"Scalar"` of $\langle\phi|H|\phi\rangle$, `ComplexExpand`, hand to `NMinimize` or `MinValue`) is real and works: measured live, the closed-form cost of a two-layer hardware-efficient ansatz against a $ZZ$-chain Hamiltonian is built in about two seconds or less up to five qubits, and `NMinimize` on the four-qubit cost (8 parameters) reaches the exact ground energy $-3$ in 51 s (emitting `NMinimize::cvmit` on the way). But the expression grows as the state does: 797 leaves at $n=2$, 907,009 leaves at $n=6$ (12 s to build), and the optimization gives out first: the same `NMinimize` call on the five-qubit cost (10 parameters, 142,465 leaves) did not finish within a 600 s cap. There is no packaged numeric cost function: composing one by hand (substitute numbers into the circuit, apply, contract bra-operator-ket) does work and stays nearly flat in system size (measured 0.3-0.7 s per evaluation from four to ten qubits, so the six-qubit wall is a wall of the symbolic route only), but half a second per cost call through the object layer is too slow to drive an optimizer, and each user reinvents the loop by hand (the tutorial's own H3+ example does, via a hand-written `meanEnergy`).

**F7. The exact metric tensor stops at toy size.** `FubiniStudyMetricTensor` differentiates the full symbolic state vector and simplifies: measured 0.6 s at 4 parameters, 6.6 s at 6, 74 s at 8. The block-diagonal measured approximation (the form hardware uses) exists but works for only one circuit shape: `FubiniStudyMetricTensorLayers` reconstructs layers by pattern-matching gate *labels* (rewriting `Subscript["C","NOT"]` strings, dropping anything unrecognized, assuming alternating parametrized/entangling blocks, `:419-446`), so a circuit that deviates from the lesson's template is sliced wrongly with no message. Verified live: a two-layer circuit whose final block is a parametrized layer (the commonest hardware-efficient shape, and the shape the tutorial's own first example uses) comes back as a single layer, the last one silently dropped, because the slicer assumes a trailing entangling block.

**F8. The parameter-shift functions are exhibits, not tools, and one of them shipped broken.** `SPSRGradientValues`/`ASPSRGradientValues` implement the stochastic shift rule for one hardcoded scenario: a user-supplied generator function of a single parameter, a fixed default measurement operator on exactly two qubits, a fixed $\theta$ sweep. They answer "reproduce the plot from the paper," not "differentiate my circuit." At the audit anchor, `SPSRGradientValues`, called exactly as the tutorial calls it, terminated with `$RecursionLimit::reclim` and returned `TerminatedEvaluation[RecursionLimit]`, while `ASPSRGradientValues` worked (a reduced sweep returns its value table in ~7 s); the tutorial's stored outputs date from an older kernel, and no test would have caught the regression (F10). The cause was the bare string `pauli` spliced into `Exp[I φ pauli]` inside the circuit (`:485` at the anchor), where ASPSR wraps `QuantumOperator[pauli]`; this audit's finding was fixed on `main` during its verification (`4cb27f78`, `4d36041e`: every SPSR gate now built as an explicit `MatrixExp` of a `QuantumOperator`, plus two regression tests). The underlying kernel weakness survives the fix: the minimal reproducer `QuantumCircuitOperator[{Exp[I φ "PauliX"]}, "Parameters" -> {φ}][]` still aborts the same way at current `main`, and the abort leaves the `wolframscript` exit status at 0, so scripted runs report success. QF still has no general parameter-shift gradient of a `QuantumCircuitOperator`, despite the shift rule being a one-line consequence of machinery it already owns.

**F9. There is no problem layer at all.** Nothing turns a graph, a QUBO matrix, an integer program, or a portfolio into a cost Hamiltonian; nothing turns a measurement outcome back into a cut, an assignment, or a portfolio. The tutorial types the Pauli strings by hand for its first Max-Cut instance and assembles them with `StringReplacePart` for the larger ones, and for the one chemistry example it shells out to PennyLane to obtain the Hamiltonian matrix. The old `QuantumHamiltonianOperator` subsystem that once carried `"Ising"` and `"TransverseIsing"` constructors is dead in-source (flagged "decapitated" at `QuantumHamiltonialOperator.m:1`), and no named operator or circuit replaces it: `$QuantumOperatorNames` has no Ising entry, and `$QuantumCircuitOperatorNames` (44 entries) contains no QAOA, no hardware-efficient ansatz, no mixer.

**F10. Documentation state.** The tutorial is substantive (VQA basics through VQLS) and is the only documentation. It ships with an internal editorial note still in the text ("**remove this, focus only on our convention...**", in the HHL section), an unbounded `NMaximize` example whose optimal angle comes back as $\gamma \approx -2\times10^{27}$ because nobody bounded a periodic parameter, timing outputs of 446 s and 2286 s for its own finite-difference VQLS runs, and stub metadata (`XXXX`). The two shipped tests cover one metric-tensor value and one descent convergence; nothing tests the solver, the adiabatic object, the layers, or any failure path.

**F11. The Classiq bridge works, with one wrong idiom.** `ClassiqSetup` finds a compatible Python (3.10-3.12), pip-installs `classiq`, pins `"LatestClassiqVersion" -> "0.86.0"` and force-upgrades on mismatch (a hardcoded moving target), and reports failures with `Print[ClassiqSetup::ClassiqInstallation]`, which prints the message *text* instead of issuing the message (`:302`); its Python-version probes also sit inside a `Quiet` whose suppression list carries an in-source TODO admitting it was never enumerated. The actual circuit bridge (`ClassiqQuantumState`, package-scoped in `Classiq.m`) synthesizes a state-preparation circuit through Classiq's cloud and re-imports it through the native OpenQASM reader: a genuinely useful capability, undocumented and unexported.

**F12. What already works well.** Three real assets sit underneath the weak layer. First, parametric circuits are first-class symbolic objects: `"Parameters"` declarations propagate through composition, application, and measurement, substitution by association or position is uniform, and closed-form amplitudes fall out of any circuit (of the compared platforms, only Qrisp has a partial analogue, a SymPy state-vector export with symbolic gate parameters; PennyLane, qiskit-optimization, and dimod have none). Second, the classical optimization stack is already the strongest of any quantum platform's host language, in the same kernel with no bridge: `NMinimize`'s method zoo, `LinearOptimization` solving integer programs exactly, `FindMaximumCut`, `FindShortestTour`, `FindIndependentVertexSet`, `SatisfiableQ`, convex optimization. Third, the surrounding framework supplies what a redesign needs: `"PauliDecompose"`, `"Trotterization"`, Pauli-string operator construction, `QuantumEvolve` for real annealing dynamics, qudits throughout, and precedent for solver-shaped result objects (`QuantumStateEstimate` returns a rich estimation object).

## 3. The comparison

Every mature stack, gate-based or annealing, converged on the same four layers. Reading the cloned sources:

**qiskit-optimization** (the field's reference implementation): a modeling class (`QuadraticProgram`: binary/integer/continuous variables, linear and quadratic constraints, translators to and from the classical modeling languages docplex, gurobipy, LP files); converters (`IntegerToBinary`, `InequalityToEquality`, penalty folding, `QuadraticProgramToQubo`) and the two-way `to_ising`/`from_ising` translation; solvers as interchangeable wrappers over one interface (`MinimumEigenOptimizer` around QAOA, sampling VQE, or exact diagonalization; recursive QAOA; warm-start QAOA; Grover adaptive search; a splitting method for mixed binary/continuous problems; quantum random-access encoding with rounding; classical baselines including Goemans-Williamson and CPLEX/Gurobi behind the same interface); and interpretation (every result returns decoded variable values, objective value, status, and the full sample list; each of the 13 concrete application classes, Max-Cut through vehicle routing, has `to_quadratic_program` and `interpret`).

**PennyLane**: cost-Hamiltonian builders that return the matching mixer with the problem (`maxcut`, plus `max_independent_set`, `min_vertex_cover`, and `max_clique`, the latter three each with a `constrained=True` variant that swaps penalties for a constraint-preserving mixer), layer builders, native automatic differentiation with parameter-shift rules, and a fermionic module plus chemistry layer feeding VQE.

**Qrisp**: a QAOA library organized per problem (QUBO, MaxSat, E3Lin2, coloring with one-hot encodings, set cover, portfolio rebalancing) with benchmarking utilities (approximation-ratio tracking) and QIRO, the quantum-informed recursive variant.

**D-Wave dimod**: the annealing side's modeling objects (binary, constrained, and discrete quadratic models, higher-order models, symbolic arithmetic on binary variables) feeding samplers and hybrid solvers.

Against this grid, QF today has fragments of the solver layer only, and its one full solver (`QuantumLinearSolve`) addresses a linear-algebra problem rather than the optimization stack. Nothing in QF corresponds to layers 1 (model), 2 (encode), or 4 (interpret and benchmark). Conversely, none of those platforms has QF's three assets: closed-form parametric states, an exact integer-programming and global-optimization suite in the same session, and symbolic operator algebra (bosonic shipped, fermionic planned). PennyLane and Qiskit differentiate numerically; they cannot hand the user the cost function as an expression, prove a landscape symmetry, or return $-\sqrt{2}$ rather than $-1.41421$.

## 4. What a WL-best design looks like

The design thesis follows from the audit: do not rebuild qiskit-optimization's breadth, and do not compete on shot-based simulation throughput. Build the instrument the field lacks: **the exact end of quantum optimization, with classical baselines built into every answer.** The two things WL is uniquely positioned to do are (a) closed forms where everyone else has floats, and (b) an honest classical comparison in the same kernel call, which is precisely the discipline the community's own survey and benchmark library demand (Q6). Everything below is phrased as the physics it enables, with the core-kernel idioms (named constructors, typed objects with properties) that the current sub-package ignores and the rest of QF does well.

### 4.1 Repair the foundation (small, immediate)

- Put the optimization symbols on the load path or in the `Symbols` contract so a bare load sees them (F1); until then every other improvement is invisible too.
- Switch generated parameters to formal symbols (`\[FormalTheta]` indexed), killing the session-capture bug (F2) while keeping the ergonomic layer sugar.
- Make the natural-gradient step do what its equation says: `PseudoInverse` (or `LinearSolve` with an explicit regularizer), plus a gradient-norm stopping test (F3).
- Fix the adiabatic degeneracy logic (F4): a closing gap at the endpoint between states inside one symmetry sector of the solution space is the normal case, not an error; report the gap to the first level outside the final ground space (or per symmetry sector), and add a numeric sweep path so the analysis survives past two qubits without Root-object algebra.
- Move the locking demo to example material; issue messages with `Message`, not `Print` (F11); delete the `$ModuleNumber` resets (F5); ship the tutorial without the leftover editorial note, with bounded periodic parameters in optimizer examples (F10).

### 4.2 Build the missing middle in QF's own idiom

The named-constructor system is QF's native answer to layers 1-3, and its catalogs are where users already look:

- **Hamiltonians as named operators.** `QuantumOperator["Ising"[J, h]]`, `"TransverseIsing"[...]` (resurrecting the dead subsystem's names as ordinary named operators), `"MaxCut"[graph]`, `"QUBO"[Q]`, `"HigherOrderBinary"[tensor]` for the cubic-and-up terms QUBO cannot hold, all returning sums of Pauli strings with the constant offset tracked. The inverse direction is one function: interpret a computational-basis outcome as an assignment of the original variables with its objective value.
- **A modeling entry point that leans on WL, not on a new class.** Users already write objectives and constraints as WL expressions over variables; `LinearOptimization` and friends already parse them. One converter, expression + variable domains in, cost Hamiltonian + penalty report + decoder out, covers what `QuadraticProgram` does in Python with far less machinery, and gives the penalty weights symbolically so the user can see exactly when a penalty term stops dominating.
- **Ansatz circuits as named circuits.** `QuantumCircuitOperator["QAOA"[H_C, p]]` (phase layers via the existing `"Trotterization"` pieces, mixer choice as an option including the XY and bit-flip constraint-preserving mixers, warm-start state as an option), `"HardwareEfficient"[n, layers]` wrapping the existing layer sugar. These turn the tutorial's page of hand-written construction into one line and put QAOA where users look for circuits.
- **One expectation-and-gradient primitive.** A numeric fast path for $\langle\phi(\theta)|H|\phi(\theta)\rangle$ (substitute, contract by tensor network, never expand symbolically) with the parameter-shift gradient as a property of the circuit, replacing the demonstration functions (F8) and removing the six-qubit wall (F6) for numeric work while keeping the symbolic route for small systems.
- **One driver returning a typed result.** A single function (working name `QuantumVariationalSolve`, mirroring how `QuantumStateEstimate` already returns a rich estimation object) that takes Hamiltonian + ansatz + method, runs the classical optimizer of choice (`NMinimize` methods, the repaired descent functions, or exact `MinValue` when the cost is small enough to close), and returns an object whose properties are the energy, the parameters, the state, the optimization trajectory, the decoded solution when the Hamiltonian came from a problem, and, by default, **the classical baseline**: the exact or heuristic classical answer (`LinearOptimization`, `FindMaximumCut`, simulated annealing) with the approximation ratio. Benchmark honesty as a property of every result, not a separate workflow.

### 4.3 The distinctive layer (what nobody else can ship)

- **Exact mode as a first-class method.** Closed-form cost expressions, `MinValue` certificates, symbolic angle solutions at depth one and two, landscape symmetry and degeneracy reports. QAOA's depth-1 Max-Cut expectation has a known closed form per edge neighborhood; producing it symbolically for an arbitrary graph is a WL-only capability and directly serves the parameter-concentration literature (Q7).
- **Annealing analysis worth citing.** The repaired gap object (symmetry-resolved, numeric sweeps, arbitrary schedules) plus true dynamics through `QuantumEvolve` (which already integrates time-dependent Hamiltonians well), giving diabatic-error curves against sweep time. Qudit cost encodings (one-hot constraints become single qudit levels; QF is one of the few frameworks where a qutrit register is native) are a genuinely open playground here.
- **The fermionic layer as the VQE source.** The existing plan (`OngoingProjects/SecondQuantization-FutureProjects/Fermionic-SecondQuantization/README.md`) already specifies `FermionicHamiltonian[h, V]` and the Jordan-Wigner bridge; landing it removes the tutorial's PennyLane dependence (F9, Q4) and connects optimization to the symbolic operator algebra no competing platform has.

### 4.4 Non-goals

Hardware-loop throughput (sampling-based optimization runs on devices) belongs to the existing Qiskit/IBM bridge, not to new kernel code. The thirteen-application catalog, the splitting solvers for mixed continuous problems, and commercial-solver wrappers are qiskit-optimization's territory and WL's convex/integer stack already covers the classical part natively. `QuantumLinearSolve` should be repositioned honestly as a teaching implementation of the multiplexer encoding (its documentation should show the cost data above) rather than grown further; the HHL material stays a worked example.

### 4.5 Order of leverage

1. Foundation repairs (4.1): days of work, and everything else is invisible or untrustworthy without them.
2. Named Hamiltonians + QAOA circuit + decoder (4.2, first three items): this alone moves QF from "no support" to "usable" on Q1-Q3, reusing machinery that exists.
3. The numeric expectation/gradient primitive: unlocks $n > 6$ and honest optimizer comparisons.
4. The driver with built-in classical baseline: the visible product, and the one aligned with where the field says the frontier is (Q6).
5. Exact-mode showcases and the annealing object: the material that differentiates WL publicly.
